#### Introduction to Taliria

**Taliria** was a verdant and halcyon kingdom until its collapse in its third dynasty during what was known as the Goblin Wars.  At the time of its collapse, it had spread across a continent bordering two seas and having assimilated seven disparate and variegated provinces; this document focuses on **Oasis Scourge**, a former province of Taliria, and its neighbor to the north, **Wyvern's Weald**.  

The seven provinces of the former kingdom were:

- **Oasis Scourge** — *the Scourge*
- **Wyvern's Weald** — *the Weald*
- **Northern Forest Edge** — *the Edge Frontier*
- **Ashen Pale** — *the Pale*
- **Middle Vale** — *the Vale*
- **Verdant Steppes** — *the Steppes*
- **Coral Pass** — *Coral*

- **NOTE Wyvern's Mark**: Wyvern's Mark was ceded from The Weald to the Oasis Scourge fiefdom by the third former Talirian King.  This mistakenly was to make The Scourge stronger and secure the Talirian borders from incursions in the southern lands.  Many of the folk of The Mark see the fall of the Talirian Kingdom as an opportunity to rejoin their Weald brethren. While this is disputed amongst many of them, for it could unite the Scourge against the Weald, there is a long and deep love of the northern Weald, and a return to its fold.


#### Background of the Talirian Kingdom  
For more than four hundred years, the seven provinces of Taliria stood as one, ruled by a succession of Alderdell dynasties. It was no small thing. The land itself does not invite unity. Massive interior ranges divide Taliria into distinct provinces, creating natural barriers that shaped separate fiefdoms, cultures, and trade routes over centuries. The largest mountain chain cuts across the heart of the continent. Two long western ranges form a broad central corridor that became the core of Talirian identity — a rugged valley lorded over by the Wyverns. Mountain passes control commerce and military movement, and many regions developed independently behind these natural defenses. Each province has coasts and abundant trade, though Coral Pass has long dominated the seas, from the Embankment in the north all the way down to the swampish reaches of the HobBog lowlands.  
  
  

#### The Provinces  

To the **north** lie two vastly different lands: the **Northern Forest Edge** and **Ashen Pale**. The Edge Frontier is the Northwestern province and one of the largest in both land and people; marked by a central mountain chain that feeds waters into the valleys and swamps below.  It shares the Embankment with Ashen Pale - a large body of water dotted with islands and passes that open out into the ocean.  The Ashen Pale is a land of old ruin and older silences, where the war is said to have ended and something unnamed endured after. Few who pass into the Pale return unchanged, and fewer still speak clearly of what they saw there.

To the **west** lies **Wyvern's Weald**, a valley that tests the mettle of those who enter the Wyvern's Run — a contest held every four years to cross the southern Weald and outlast its Wyverns and other dangers lurking in the forests and open fields. Those who survive are inducted into the Cragwold Halls and are memorialized forever.

At the **center** of Taliria sits **Middle Vale**, home of the forest Alderdell and the seat of Talirian power. It was here that the Goblin General — elevated to the title of The Crownless, equal to the Goblin King himself for the duration of the incursion — met the Talirian King in battle and defeated him.

To the **east**, the **Verdant Steppes** roll from the Vale's mountain range to the eastern seas. Once bucolic meadowland inhabited largely by the Glenkin, the Steppes were where the Crownless General continued his conquest after the Vale fell — while the Goblin King turned north from Oasis Scourge through the Weald. The Glenkin were enslaved during the incursion and lost much of their culture, and most of their farmland, to the war.

To the **southwest** lies **Oasis Scourge** and its Shatterlands — a maze-like fracture of broken stone that borders the Red Frontier, the great southern desert beyond the Talirian lands. It was from these deserts that the Goblin armies emerged thirty-five years ago, scouring the Shatterlands and battling through the frontier passes of the Scourge. The border province remains culturally shaped by the memory of invasion.

To the **far east**, across open water, sits **Coral Pass** — a long, claw-shaped island whose people, the Coralfolk, rule most of the sea between the mainland and the open ocean.


#### The Fall

The Goblin Wars marked the end of the Talirian Kingdom. With the Talirian King defeated and the unified throne broken, each province fell into its own politics — though every one of them is still shaped, in ways the chroniclers are only beginning to understand, by the friendship between the Goblin King and the Talirian King.

That friendship is its own story, and not one this document is ready to tell. For now, it is enough to say that the two were friends before fate interceded, and that the manner of the kingdom's fall cannot be understood without remembering it.

The events described elsewhere in this document take place **thirty-five years after** the invasion began. The kingdom is gone. The provinces remain.


#### Races of the Talirian Kingdom

Taliria is home to seven major folk:

Core Folk of Oasis Scourge:
- **HobBogs**
- **Cragwolds**
- **Humans**
- **Glenkin**

Peripheral Folk:
- **Tordwergs**
- **Alderdell**
- **Coralfolk** of Coral Pass (In the Oasis Scourge, most commonly found in the harbor settlements)

The provinces of Taliria vary widely in racial composition. Some, such as Oasis Scourge, are heavily mixed along the coasts while remaining culturally homogeneous in the deep interiors. Others, like the Vale, were once cosmopolitan but later fractured along older ethnic and provincial lines.

The sections that follow address the peoples of Oasis Scourge specifically; the remaining provinces are detailed in their own regional volumes.

##### Cragwolds

<a href="cragwolds.html" onclick="openPage('cragwolds.html'); return false;">

Cragwolds, known among themselves as the Wyraks, are a broad and enduring mountain folk shaped by the escarpments, cliff valleys, and stone ridges of western Taliria. They are found throughout the Weald, the Northern Forest Edge, and the Middle Vale, where their fortress settlements and terraced strongholds overlook the deep passes and canyon roads of the interior. Many possess heavy brows, thick frames, and pronounced tusks, traits long associated with the harsher mountain bloodlines of the west.

Unlike the marsh-bound HobBogs, the Cragwolds are deeply devoted to history, philosophy, and ancestral memory. Their halls preserve centuries of recorded disputes, oral epics, and stone-carved chronicles passed from elder to elder across generations.

In Oasis Scourge, Cragwold communities are concentrated primarily within the mountain regions of the Weald Mark and Ternhaven. The link above shares more about these highland folk.


##### Glenkins

[**Go to Glenkins of Oasis Scourge Main Page**](glenkins.html)


The Glenkin are one of the oldest agrarian folk of Taliria, descended from the fertile river plains and fisheries of the Verdant Steppes where dense communal settlements flourished long before the rise of the southern kingdoms. Small-bodied and broad-built, they became renowned throughout the eastern provinces for their mastery of floodplain farming, fisheries, orchards, and canal trade.

Large Glenkin populations settled the bucolic Glentali Lake region of Oasis Scourge where they fed both The Scourge and the neighboring Weald through generations of patient agricultural labor. The Goblin Wars shattered much of this world. Entire communes collapsed beneath invasion, famine, enslavement, and forced migration, leaving the Glenkin deeply fractured between mountain refugees, displaced lake-folk, and Pale Hymn-aligned communes resettled under Deyve influence following the wars.

### HobBogs of Salt-Willow

#### Overview

The HobBogs of Salt-Willow, known as FogWisps, are a western bog folk of the Oasis Scourge, where reedwater runs through mangrove shadow and the islets drift over tangled roots. To be born among them is to inherit a living memory: songs carried through fog, warnings passed quietly between kin, and the instinctive understanding that the ground beneath one's feet may never fully settle.

FogWisps stand low to the earth, lean and moss-skinned, with expressive swamp-ears and long dexterous fingers shaped by labor and craft. Their balance appears almost unnatural to outsiders, but it is simply the inheritance of generations raised on uncertain ground. They move easily where others stumble, sink, or lose their way. Their hearing is sharp. Their night-sense steady. 

FogWisps are rarely seen without their poles, which are standard tools of daily life.  They engrave their bog-oak poles with mire-rich carvings and adorn them with shells and alligator teeth. A pole may steady the body, test the depth of root-shadowed water, guide a skiff, or serve in fishing. In Salt-Willow, such a thing is not merely an object. It is an extension of movement, caution, and survival.

They build their lives from the swamp, reading its waterways and tree lines. Reeds become shelter. Willowwood becomes craft. Fishing, boats, and floating walkways become the foundation of community. Their homes rest upon drifting swamp islands, which outsiders often mistake for fragile places. To the FogWisps, a home that moves with the water is wiser than one that breaks against it. 

FogWisps do not merely live in the swamp; they resemble it. Their thoughts, customs, stories, and agreements move the way water moves around roots — bending, circling, returning, and finding passage where a straighter folk would break. To outsiders this can feel evasive. To these HobBogs, it is the natural shape of wisdom.

Salt-Willow's folk are communal without being faceless. Kinship, memory, and obligation bind them together, yet individuality is deeply prized. It is considered a great sadness for one to possess a gift, a strangeness, or hidden beauty and never express it within the community.  While hermitdom exists within Salt-Willow, it is regarded as a curious and unvalued lifestyle.

Salt-Willow history is not written so much as remembered. Stories move through gatherings, songs, family retellings, ferry-talk, and ritual performance. Details may shift from one telling to the next, but the emotional truth remains. Among the Salt-Willow HobBogs, memory is considered alive — carried by the people, reshaped by need, and renewed whenever it is spoken aloud.

Even their games reflect this philosophy. Children change rules as they play, turning simple contests into acts of balance, cleverness, laughter, and negotiation. Adult life follows the same rhythm. Agreements are honored, but they breathe. Customs are respected, but they bend.

To outsiders, this can seem slippery or inconsistent.

To the HobBogs of Salt-Willow, it is survival refined into culture.

> "Shiftiness is shelter that knows how to walk."

For the FogWisps, survival belongs not to those who reign over the marsh, but to those who listen to it. The wise know when water is warning them. They know when silence means danger. They know which old songs carry truth, and which paths are not paths at all.  To be a FogWisp is to swamp.

#### The Land

Salt-Willow stretches across northern freshwater cypress marshes and southern mangrove labyrinths, fed by the joining of mountain runoff and swelling sea currents. Between them lies a long open seam of reedwater and mudflat, marking the divide between the region's biomes, creatures, and ways of life.

The southern mangrove maze forms the commercial heart of the region. Large fortified island settlements guard trade routes against raiders, pirates, and eastern incursions. Skiffs, ferries, and trading craft move through these shadowed waterways, carrying foodstuffs and hides, carved goods and botanicals, poisons drawn from the deep marsh. These goods move outward to The Scourge, Coral Pass harbor towns, and the wider provinces of the former Talirian kingdom.

The northern cypress marshes form the heart of Salt-Willow's swamping communities. These boglands feed much of Salt-Willow and supply settlements beyond its borders. Families here live close to the water, raising their homes upon marsh masses and cypress-shadowed banks, on islets that shift with the season and sloughs that drain without warning.

Salt-Willow is divided into bogs and smaller boglets. A bog is a cluster of large islets, sloughs, reedbanks, and marsh-mounds connected by wooden bridges, planks, skiffs, and muddy paths. Some boglets hold only a handful of families, while the greater bogs contain whole communities with workshops and trading houses, ferry points and shrines, and defensive posts built up over generations.

Four major bogs form the old backbone of Salt-Willow: Willowmarch, Mossmere, Reedveil, and Greyfen. Together they supported the standing defenders, the naval skiffs, and the unified trade outlets that once made Salt-Willow one of the great wetland powers of the Oasis Scourge.

Deep in the northern cypress, near the crashing waterfalls of Highwater, lies Highwater Islet, where the Fen Mother keeps her hidden seat. Far to the southeast, near the open sea, Saltrest forms the largest and most outward-facing island of Salt-Willow. Its U-shaped harbor shelters military holds, trading docks, allied ships, and the taverns where swampfolk, sailors, merchants, and envoys gather for trade, mead, rumor, and song.

#### The Goblin Wars

Salt-Willow's strength began to fracture during the Goblin Wars. As the Crownless King carved his way east toward Middle Vale, the FogWisps of Salt-Willow and the Cragwolds of the Weald fought to slow the invasion through the swamps and mountain passes. They could not stop the advance, but their resistance bought precious time for Middle Vale and the fleeing Talirian court.

The cost was severe. Salt-Willow's waterways became militarized. Trade routes collapsed. Skiff patrols became war patrols. Families were scattered between fortified bogs, refugee islets, and hidden marsh camps. What had once been a living web of trade, kinship, and ritual movement hardened into a defensive maze.

The war also deepened the old enmity between Salt-Willow and Dusk Reed. Though both peoples descend from ancient HobBog memory, they had long since become enemies of water, trade, and belief. Dusk Reed sought passage to the outer sea, while Salt-Willow worked with Coral Pass to prevent it. By guarding the western waterways, blocking canal excavations, and controlling the approaches to the coastal channels, Salt-Willow kept its eastern rival trapped within the interior marshes.

That containment did not end the threat. It merely changed its shape.

In the decades after the Goblin Wars, Dusk Reed expanded westward through Pale Hymn alliances and the growing settlements around Greyharbor Downs and Glentali Lake. Freed Glenkin labor, reshaped by Pale Hymn doctrine, helped build new agricultural power beyond the swamp. Salt-Willow found itself pressured not only from Dusk Reed's marshes, but from the farms, roads, and frontier towns rising around its borders.

Ten years before the present day, Greyfen was destroyed during a western incursion. Its fall sent refugees into Mossmere, weakened the old defensive order, and left Reedveil exposed to the radical currents spreading through the mudflats. Salt-Willow survived, but no longer as the unified wetland power it had once been.

Salt-Willow became a refuge for scattered Dellari who had escaped Dusk Reed or fled back from the mountain refuges with nowhere else to go. The FogWisps did not forget that the Dellari had once fed the lake basin, nor that Dusk Reed and its Ashverin proxies now stood where the old Glentali Glenkin had lived. These refugees brought grief, warnings, and memory into the bogs, deepening Salt-Willow's hatred of the eastern marsh.

Now Salt-Willow struggles after the collapse of its Talirian kingdom.  Willowmarch clings to renewed trade and the fading structures of the old world. Mossmere hardens itself for the invasion it believes will come again. Reedveil listens too closely to dangerous promises from the frontier. And Saltrest, still loud with trade, song, and foreign voices, remains the outward face of a people struggling to hold the swamp together.

#### Lore

The elders of Salt-Willow are guardians of the lore, carrying old stories through the raised fire pits of the bogs, where each telling is reshaped by the voices, troubles, humor, and memory of their folk. One of the oldest is The Dryad, the Cypress, and the First Salt-Willow.


<div class="lore-scroll" markdown="1">

In the elder-tale, the southern cypress once stood ankle-deep in sweet marshwater, tall, solemn, and very proud of how ancient they sounded when they complained. Then the sea began creeping inland. Salt touched their roots, and the cypress groaned so loudly that frogs abandoned three ponds just to hear themselves think.

Their dryad listened for a while.

Then she slapped a cypress trunk with both hands and said, "Enough. I have heard rocks with livelier courage."

The cypress shuddered with offense.

"We are trees," they said. "Moving is dramatic."

"Then be dramatic northward," said the dryad.

So the cypress began their slow march toward Highwater, where the northern willows grew beneath clean mist and crashing falls. The willows saw the cypress coming, muddy, moss-draped, and full of grievance.

One willow leaned to another and whispered, "They smell of wet sorrow."

A cypress answered, "And you smell of vanity and waterfall."

The argument lasted until moonrise.

The dryad stood between them, hands on her hips, swamp-mud up to her knees, patience floating away with the gnats.

"You all have roots and somehow lack sense," she said.

Then she pointed south.

"You," she told the willows, "come see what frightened the cypress."

The willows rustled excuses. Their leaves needed mist. Their roots preferred sweetness. Their branches felt arranged just so. At last, one young willow stepped forward, slender and silver-leaved.

"I will go," he said, "because this arguing has become embarrassing."

The dryad liked him immediately.

She led him south through the cypress maze, past reed pools, frog logs, and mangrove shade, until they reached the brackish seam where fresh runoff met the sea-pulled channels. The young willow placed one root into the salt-touched mud and curled every leaf in horror.

The mangroves laughed so hard their roots clicked.

"First taste?" they asked.

The willow almost pulled away. Then the tide moved through him. Beneath the sharpness, he felt the sea carrying shells, storms, far coasts, and old songs from beyond the marsh. Behind him, Highwater still sang through the northern current. For one breath, both waters entered the same root.

The dryad saw his leaves still.

"There," she said more softly. "You heard it."

The willow bent lower.

"Sweetwater remembers," he said.

"Good," said the dryad.

"Saltwater carries."

"Better."

"And fog joins the two."

The dryad grinned. "Now you are becoming useful."

She pressed her palm to his trunk and gave him her swamp-gift: bark that could bear brine, roots that could sort sweetness from sting, and leaves that could drink dawn mist when the mud grew harsh.

By morning, the willow still stood.

By the next dusk, his bark had paled.

By the third dawn, silver-green leaves opened over the southern channel, and frogs gathered beneath him as though they had advised the whole affair from the beginning.

The dryad named him Salt-Willow, first of the willows to drink both waters.

The cypress learned to settle where Highwater cooled their roots. The willows learned to send their boldest children south. The mangroves claimed the plan had always been obvious. The frogs agreed loudly with everyone.


</div>

From the old tale comes the FogWisp pattern: live through change, mark the change, and carry the mark forward. The first Salt-Willow drank both waters and became a new tree. A FogWisp does the same in smaller ways. A hard crossing earns a mark. A clever bargain earns a mark. Even a feast that nearly failed, or a tale that proved worth keeping, earns its place in memory.

The old songs evolve. The histories carried in dialect shift with each retelling. Even the jokes change shape. But the emotional core of the story remains sacred.

#### Swamping

Swamping forms the economic and cultural backbone of Salt-Willow. It is the daily work of drawing life from marsh, channel, reedbed, rootline, and drifting islet.

A skilled swamper knows where fish gather, where reeds cut clean, where fungi rise after damp heat, where traps should sit, and which waters carry a skiff home before dusk. This knowledge feeds the bogs and supplies trade through Saltrest, Coral Pass, and the wider Scourge.

The northern cypress marshes provide much of Salt-Willow's food and craft wealth. Fish and hides, fungi and medicines, poisons worked from marsh plants, binding fiber from reeds, and the skiff timber and raftwood that keep the boglets connected — these goods move through family hands first, then outward through the southern mangrove channels.

Swamping also teaches a FogWisp how to read the land. Every channel has habits. Every stump, root-tangle, ferry bend, and half-sunk log can serve as a marker. A strange current may reveal a changed bed beneath the water. A patch of reeds may show where fish are moving. A quiet slough may hold traps or eggs, herbs or something that should be left alone.

Most FogWisps live upon bog islands joined by kinship, skiff-runs, bridge planks, and remembered paths. A boglet survives because its families know which waters to share, which crossings to mend, which stores to fill, and which routes to keep open before flood season.

Good swamping earns deep respect. It turns attention into food, patience into trade, and memory into survival. To swamp well is to belong well.

A pole gathers that memory first. Root-lines and current spirals, shell ties and family notches, reed-cuts and alligator teeth — these show where a FogWisp has traveled and what the swamp has taught them. The pole begins as a tool, then becomes a record carried in the hand.

Skiffs carry the same memory across water. Their hulls are painted with willow branches and family signs, frog-eyes and current lines, and small jokes tucked beneath the rail. A good skiff mark tells the boglet where the boat belongs, who shaped it, and what sort of trouble its owner enjoys surviving.

Around the raised fire pits, these marks are renewed. Elders tell the old tales. Families carve fresh lines into poles. Young hands paint new signs onto skiff boards. A clever mark earns laughter and spreads by morning. A solemn mark stays with one family for generations.

#### Morality and Social Bonds

FogWisp morality begins with the boglet. A thing is judged first by whom it touches — whether that person is kin, a welcomed guest, a stranger passing through, or something closer to a threat. The same act may carry different meaning depending on the bond around it. To a FogWisp, morality lives in relationship, obligation, memory, and return.

Family goods are held close. A trap line or a fish rack, a good skiff or a drying hide — these may belong to one household, yet they also sit within the wider life of the boglet. When a struggling family takes from a richer family's waters, the act can be folded into kinship. Need creates its own claim. The debt may ripen later in labor or loyalty, in protection or craft, or some future kindness brought back when fortune turns.

Strangers live by a different current. A careless wayfarer in a tavern, a merchant flashing coin, or a sailor boasting over too much drink may become fair sport for quick hands. FogWisps admire clever taking when it is clean, quiet, and well-timed. A stolen purse may be shameful in one place and funny in another. What matters is the bond being touched.

Kinship sets the sacred line. To cheat one's own family, betray a boglet, abandon a shared crossing, or sell out a skiff-run to enemies carries deep shame. FogWisps can forgive idleness, foolishness, hunger, mischief, and wandering. They have a harder time forgiving a bond deliberately broken.

Generosity often moves through the same channels as mischief. A FogWisp may steal from a drunken traveler at Saltrest and leave the spoils in the family stilt before dawn. A head family may control the best fishing bends and still let a hungry household draw from them for a season. A young idler may live off the boglet for years, then one day repay the debt through courage, craft, warning, or sacrifice.

This gives Salt-Willow a morality that feels slippery to outsiders and sensible to its own folk. Property bends around belonging. Debt moves through time. Loyalty matters more than possession. A FogWisp may take from the wider world with a grin, then give everything back to the boglet when the water rises.

#### Speech and Dialect

FogWisp speech is rhythmic, emotional, and shaped by the pressure of the moment. Meaning often rides in cadence, pause, repetition, and emphasis. A FogWisp may say the same phrase twice and mean two different things, depending on where the breath lands.

Their dialect favors soft turns, added endings, and possessive phrasing. Words often gather small sounds at the edges: I's, you's, takes, coulds, wents, young'uns. These endings give the speech a rolling, bog-born rhythm, like a skiff nudged through reeds.

FogWisps speak with metaphor because the swamp speaks that way. A lazy cousin may be "a frog waiting for the fly to apologize." A dangerous silence may be "water holding its breath." A clever thief may be "quick as a reed-shadow and twice as pleased." Even practical speech often carries image, humor, and sideways meaning.

Emotion changes the shape of the dialect. A calm FogWisp lets words drift and curl. A worried one repeats, circles, and tests the thought aloud. An angry one sharpens the endings, letting the final sounds arrive late and hard. The hiss at the end of a word can carry more threat than the word itself.

Conversation among FogWisps is rarely clean and straight. They interrupt with corrections, jokes, old sayings, and small challenges. Agreement may come as a grunt, a proverb, a laugh, or a story that seems to wander before finding its point. A FogWisp who answers too directly may be seen as frightened, hurried, or trying to hide the shape of their thought.

Their affection is equally indirect. Kin praise through teasing, complaint, and practical notice. A father telling his son, "You's wake late," may be scolding, caring, and inviting him into the day all at once. A sister painting a willow on a brother's skiff may say more than a formal blessing ever could.

A few common turns of FogWisp speech include:

"I's my own I."
Said by one asserting independence, often with more pride than proof.

"That's an odds."
Said when something feels wrong, strange, lucky, dangerous, or worth watching.

"What drifts loose, drifts asking."
Said of unattended goods, loose coin, stray boats, and opportunities.

"The bog hears it first."
Said when rumor, weather, trouble, or guilt has already begun to move.

To speak like a FogWisp is to move through thought as they move through water: circling, testing, listening, joking, and letting meaning arrive by current.

FogWisps use distinct terms of address for family. A father or father figure is called a **swampa**. A mother or mother figure is a **swamma**. A brother is a **bogga**. A sister is a **sella**. An aunt is a **mossa**. An uncle is a **mossbog**. A child — one's own or a young'un within the boglet — may be called a **tad** or **tadpole**, though *young'un* is the more common address in daily speech. The term **bogkin** covers cousins, close friends, and anyone living on the same bog islets whose obligations to the household run deeper than those of a stranger.

In direct address, swampa and swamma are spoken as titles and capitalized like a name — the same way a tad might call out "Swampa" across a dock. The others — bogga, sella, mossa, mossbog, bogkin — are used as common nouns in speech. These terms carry warmth without sentimentality. A swampa telling a tad to pull the west traps is not being cold. He is including them in the work.

#### Marriage and Family

FogWisp courtship begins with curiosity. Attraction is explored through talk, travel, work, laughter, shared danger, and the small tests of daily swamp life. A clever exchange at a ferry bend, a painted sign on a skiff, a shared song beside a raised fire pit, or a steady hand during a difficult crossing may carry more weight than a formal declaration.

Love among the FogWisps is understood as discovery. Two HobBogs learn each other by moving through the world together. They watch how the other handles hunger, fear, weather, debt, family, strangers, wealth, and sudden trouble. A beautiful voice may begin a courtship. A steady pole-hand may deepen it. A good joke at the right moment may save it.

Newly bonded couples often depart on wandering journeys known simply as Quests. A Quest may last a few weeks, a season, or several years, depending on the pair and the waters that take them. Some travel through nearby boglets. Others journey toward Saltrest, Coral Pass, Glentali Lake, or the outer markets. The purpose is understanding: to see how love behaves beyond familiar planks, family eyes, and the easy comforts of home.

Wealth matters in these bonds. A FogWisp joining is often marked by bog-gifts exchanged between families — carved poles and skiff shares, smoked stores and fine hides, fishing rights or trap access passed between households, or coin earned through Saltrest trade. These gifts show that affection has weight behind it. A bond supported by generous giving brings honor to both families, especially when the gifts strengthen the new household rather than merely display pride.

A Quest is remembered through marks. A couple may carve paired lines into their poles, paint a shared willow branch along a skiff, tie matching shells beneath their grips, or bring back a sign from a place that changed them. These marks become part of their household story if they remain together, and part of their individual stories if their paths later bend apart.

Marriage itself is usually a family and boglet matter. The raised fire pit becomes the center of the joining. Families gather with food and smoke-reeds, carved poles and skiff marks, and songs made by hand for this family and no other. Elders speak the names of both households and tell the story of how the pair came together, often with enough teasing to make the couple regret every foolish thing they ever did in public.

The central marriage sign is the shared crossing. The couple steps together across a plank or a skiff board, a bridge or a reed-laid path, while their families watch. The crossing means they have chosen to move through changing water as a pair. Afterward, the families feast, sing, argue over details, improve the story, and begin turning the marriage into lore before the night is over.

A strong FogWisp marriage keeps room for strangeness. Each partner remains fully themselves within the bond. A union that gives both voices more shape is admired. Love, at its best, lets each HobBog become more visible to the bog.

---

#### Children and Games  

FogWisp children grow with unusual freedom. They wander the planks, mudpaths, skiff docks, reedbeds, and family stilts with watchful kin never far away. A young'un learns by curiosity: following an older cousin to the traps, asking a pole-maker about a carving, listening beside a raised fire pit, or disappearing for half a day to watch frogs argue with flies.

Games are shaped by the swamp's changing nature. Rules bend as the water bends. A chase across planks may become a balance trial. A hiding game may turn into a test of who can cross a rootway with the quietest feet. A skiff race may suddenly become a naming contest because the fog moved strangely and one child declared the water looked "suspicious."

One favorite game is played after the morning fog lifts from the distant mountains. The peaks stand where they always stand, but the children rename them according to what the fog has done to their shapes. One morning a peak may be Bent-Back. An hour later it becomes Tooth-Top. By noon some stubborn young'un may insist it was Swampa's Thumb all along.

The joke lies in the seriousness. Children argue the names fiercely, defend them with wild confidence, then abandon them the next morning when the fog gives the mountain a new face. Elders enjoy the game because it teaches a Salt-Willow truth without making a lesson of it: the thing may remain, while the telling changes.

Other games grow from imitation. Children pretend to be proud willows, offended cypress, smug mangroves, and foolish frogs from the old tales. They invent skiff marks with mud and berry dye. They carve practice lines into fallen sticks. They hold tiny fire-pit gatherings where one child tells a story and the others interrupt until the tale becomes funnier than the teller intended.

FogWisp childhood is full of this kind of play: naming, shifting, teasing, copying, retelling, and testing the world by laughter. The games prepare young'uns for the bog's deeper rhythms. They learn balance before duty, humor before pride, and memory before certainty.

---

#### Death and Memory

FogWisps believe the dead enter into and as the fog.

At death, the final breath joins the low morning mist that drifts across Salt-Willow. The remembered dead become part of that pale veil, moving over channels, curling around stilt posts, settling near raised fire pits, and lingering in the cypress before the day grows warm.

Morning fog carries memory. Families speak softly when it lies thick over the bogs, especially after a passing. A skiff may pause before leaving. An elder may place a hand on a pole and listen. In these hours, one's beloved dead draw nearest, present in the fog itself.

#### Songs

<a style="color: lightblue !important;" href="#" onclick="window.parent.postMessage({type: 'openPage', target: 'hobbogs_salt_willow_songs.html'}, '*'); return false;">

</a>  

Salt-Willow songs make small things feel large. A cattail, a frog, a pond, a cypress grove, or a lost stretch of marsh can carry the weight of home, grief, pride, warning, and laughter.

Cattails is the song of return: wherever a FogWisp wanders, the bog still pulls them home. The Frog Who Lost His Swamp turns one creature's sorrow into a tender song about change. Toad Knights makes a pond into a kingdom, with toads standing guard like sworn defenders beneath the cattails.

The grove-songs carry sharper edges. Cypress Queen gives the swamp a clever guardian who outwits loggers with fog, misdirection, and patience. Cypress Bones turns the same world darker, with the cypress grove answering greed through warning and consequence.

Root and River is the ache beneath the happier songs. It belongs to FogWisps who feel the bog slipping beyond reach, where drink, travel, and distraction still leave the old marsh-song inside them.

Together, Salt-Willow's songs show a folk who give full meaning to the places and creatures around them. Their music is local, strange, funny, sorrowful, and fiercely attached to home. A small pond can become a kingdom. A frog can become a mourner. A tree can become a warning. A swamp can become a voice.

### HobBogs of Dusk Reed

#### Overview

The HobBogs of Dusk Reed, known as the Deyves, occupy the southeastern marsh of the Oasis Scourge. Red mineral water moves through old rot beneath the mangroves, and every crossing depends on the household that last cleared, marked, or defended it.

Deyves tend to be short and wiry, with the balance of folk who spend their lives moving from root to plank to skiff without assuming any surface will hold. Their hands carry the strength of rope-work and boat repair. Their ears are tuned to the marsh's small sounds, because a wrong splash or a silenced insect can matter more than a shouted warning.

Their skin often holds a blue-green or gray-green cast. Red mud stains the feet and hands of anyone who works the channels long enough. Older Deyves may carry the dull marks left by years near poisoned pools. The marsh extracts payment from those who misread it, and most Deyves carry the record.

Deyves wear close-fitted clothing so roots and nets cannot snag loose cloth. They favor dark cloth because color travels far through mangrove channels and marks a position before the wearer chooses to be seen. Reed fibers and treated hides are favored for their endurance through wet labor. A wrap patched many times may carry more value than new cloth, because it has already proved itself in the marsh.

A Deyve may wear a bone charm at the throat. The charm carries a household record: what the wearer has survived, what they still owe, and what they have been warned against entering. A shell knot may mark the channel where a household made its claim. A carved tooth may show that someone who needed answering has been answered.

A Deyve tests mud before giving it weight. They listen when the marsh has grown too quiet. Still water earns suspicion because bad pools often look harmless at first; a pool without insects, ripples, or feeding fish may be poisoned, too deep to cross, or holding something beneath the surface. A familiar voice from the wrong direction is treated as danger until proven otherwise.

The pole is the tool most Deyves learn first. It reads the water before a foot enters it. It steadies the body when a root shifts. It pushes a skiff through tight channels. The pole also keeps a snake or a stranger at distance long enough for a household to decide what comes next.

A Deyve keeps a knife close because the same blade cuts rope, cleans fish, and makes a stranger reconsider their approach. It belongs to the ordinary work of a place where labor and violence are separated by less distance than outsiders expect.

Deyve homes stand above dangerous water on root-platforms and raised planks. A household keeps its stores sealed and elevated, its tools in known places, and its skiff secured near the door. A grandmother may mend rope in the same doorway where she tells children which crossings still carry old trouble.

A guest under a Deyve roof is watched, fed, and answered for until the stay ends. A household that allows harm beneath its own roof loses standing, because it has shown weakness at the threshold.

Dusk Reed began as refuge before it became homeland. The bog-folk who first settled it came from older bogs farther east, driven west by conquest and by rites they would not take. The enclosed swamp held only scattered HobBogs before them, strange even to other bog-folk, but it seemed defensible at first: rich enough to sustain a growing population, hidden enough to shelter it. Then the water began its work. The channels carried a mineral stain that marked those who drank from them and waded through them over generations. The settlers changed slowly, in body and in habit.

As the population grew, the Deyves looked outward. Canals connecting Dusk Reed to the southern sea and to the waterways of Salt-Willow would have opened trade and broken the enclosure. Coral Pass refused because the red water of Dusk Reed carried something that had already changed the folk who lived in it, and Coral Pass had no desire to let it reach open sea. The Talirian court refused because an armed marsh with sea access sat too close to the province's southern flank. The canal plans died before the first cuts were dug. Dusk Reed remained enclosed.

The bog-folk were left inside the water that had already begun to alter them. Over generations, confinement turned refuge into grievance. The marsh changed their bodies, their customs, and their understanding of what the wider world had chosen to do to them.

That grievance shaped Deyve politics while ordinary life continued. A household still mends skiffs before dawn. Parents still teach children how to read water that looks too calm. Elders still argue over debt, memory, and medicine. The marsh made the Deyves guarded, though humor survives in Dusk Reed and usually lands at someone's expense. Loyalty runs toward those who have earned it.

Deyves are HobBogs. The Crownless King entered the marsh after the war, took Deyve wives, and left behind children with both bloodlines. The Mireborn carry the quick hands, crooked humor, and sideways speech of their mothers' folk, but something runs beneath it that the old bog-stories cannot name.

> "Shadiness is the refuge where strength abides."

In Dusk Reed, the proverb earns no argument. The marsh teaches its meaning to those who stay long enough to learn it.

The Deyves read the marsh the way a soldier reads enemy ground: for what it yields, where it bites, and where it can be made to serve. It feeds those who understand its habits. It kills those who mistake beauty for safety.

---


#### The Land  

Dusk Reed lies in the eastern Scourge, where mangrove roots hold the land in narrow channels and broken ground. The water carries a red stain from mineral pools and old rot. Some pools only foul the skin. Others bring fever to anyone who drinks without knowing the signs.

A Deyve thinks in crossings. One crossing may belong to a household because that house maintains it. Another may belong to a tribe because that tribe can defend it. Some crossings are left untouched because too many folk died there for the place to be worth claiming.

Most settlements stand on root-isles. Raised planks keep sleeping places above the worst water. Mud banks become landings when the channels fall low. A bank that appears empty may still have eyes above it.

Old Siltvein families hold many of the deeper root-isles. Their authority comes from crossings guarded long before the Goblin Wars. The Murkwillows came to power along stronger channels, helped by the alliance they built with the Crownless King during the war.

The Mireborn now push toward the marsh's outer edges, clearing ground for claims that reach beyond Dusk Reed. Their blood ties them to the Crownless King and to Deyve houses that chose power over the old order. The Mangleshades survive with poorer ground and fewer claims. They endure because they know which challenge is worth making and which insult must be swallowed.

Outsiders call Dusk Reed a poisoned maze. The Deyves know it as a hard inheritance. The marsh gives fish to the patient and medicine to the careful. It gives cover to those who understand when silence matters.

#### The Goblin Wars

Dusk Reed opposed the Talirian order before the Goblin Wars reached the Scourge. The old order kept the eastern marsh contained. Coral Pass guarded the coast. Middle Vale gave force to the blockade. Salt-Willow held the western approaches and gained from the arrangement.

The Murkwillow tribe saw the Crownless King as a chance to break that order. His army could weaken Taliria in a way Dusk Reed could not achieve alone. The alliance was not built on trust. It was built on shared use.

Deyve raids struck Salt-Willow during the war. Those raids drew attention away from other movements. They also proved that Dusk Reed could carry violence through channels that outsiders struggled to read. The Crownless King gained time because the marsh knew how to hide a war.

After the Goblin army broke, the Crownless King remained in Dusk Reed. He could not return safely to his old lands. In the marsh he became a fugitive ruler with dangerous value. He took Deyve wives, and his descendants became the Mireborn.

The Mireborn changed Dusk Reed. Their rise weakened older balances between tribes. The Murkwillows gained power from the new bloodline. The Siltveins lost part of their old authority. The Mangleshades were pushed farther into the margins.

Dusk Reed also learned to use the Pale Hymn. The doctrine gave captives a new order and gave Dusk Reed a way to reshape them. The Verdant Glenkin taken through war were remade as the Ashverin. Their new identity served Dusk Reed’s western design.

Glentali Lake became the proof of that design. War had damaged its fields and crossings. The returning Dellari were scattered and weak. The Ashverin entered with doctrine behind them and began to take root where Dellari memory had been broken.

Within a generation, Glentali produced food again. It did so under a new people and a new story. Dusk Reed had escaped confinement and learned how to rule through others.

#### Tradition and Lore

Deyve lore teaches survival before comfort. Stories are told beside low fires and marked door-posts. A good story tells a child when to doubt a voice. It teaches them to read the mud before answering love.

The Backward Walker is one of the oldest terrors in Dusk Reed. It moves beyond the roots with its feet turned the wrong way. It calls in a voice that should be trusted. A child may hear a dead sister. A hunter may hear a lost mate. The voice is bait, and the mud gives the warning.

<div class="lore-scroll" markdown="1">

In the old telling, a young Deyve heard his dead sister calling from beyond the roots.

She had drowned three seasons before, but her voice came soft through the mangroves.

"Bring the pole," she said. "The mud has my ankle."

His mother stood by the house-fire and told him not to answer.

The voice called again.

This time it used his childhood name.

The boy stepped to the first root and stopped. In the mud beyond it, he saw small footprints. They looked like hers. They were wrong. The toes faced him, but the heel marks led away.

His mother did not warn him again. She placed a knife in his hand and watched.

The boy followed beside the tracks instead of inside them. He read drag, weight, broken reed, and water pull. The voice grew sweeter. Then it grew angry. Then it grew hungry.

At the red pool, something rose from the mangrove shade wearing his sister's sound. Its feet bent backward under wet hair. Its mouth opened with her voice.

The boy threw the knife into the voice instead of the body.

The thing broke like rotten bark. The pool swallowed it.

When he returned, his mother pressed red mud beneath the threshold.

"Now the house knows you," she said.

From that day, the children learned to read mud before love.

</div>

The Bride Beneath the Roots belongs to another old tale. She appears near still pools before raids, fevers, or betrayals. Some households believe she warns the strong. Others believe she calls the weak into water. No version makes her safe.

These stories shape daily practice. A child learns to study footprints before trusting a cry for help. A skiff may carry a root charm against false voices. A household that survives betrayal may carve backward feet beneath the door-post. The mark tells visitors that deception came there and was answered.

#### Swamping

Swamping is the daily work of drawing life from Dusk Reed — from its channels, its roots, its creatures, and its poisons — because in this marsh, food and danger come from the same water. It is how a Deyve household eats, repairs its skiff, and learns what the marsh forgives and what it punishes, often in the same morning’s work. A good swamper learns to read both at once.

A skilled swamper reads the marsh before entering it. They know which pools carry safe water and which will foul the skin or fever the lungs, when a root will hold weight and when mud has loosened beneath a thin skin of water. The marsh teaches most of what a swamper needs to know, and corrects those who misread it.

Fish come to those who know the channels. Eels come to those who know when the water turns. Alligator meat feeds a house when the hunt holds. Red reeds yield fiber after careful cutting. Bitter fungus becomes medicine only when an elder prepares it — and a different problem entirely when prepared wrong.

Venom requires the same care. A careless poisoner can ruin a cure or kill someone the household needed. A careful one may keep the house alive through sickness, or make an enemy reconsider touching a cup.

Goods pass first through the household. The family eats before trade begins. The skiff is repaired before tribute is sent. A hidden store may matter more than a public gift when bad water ruins a season.

A broken reed shows recent passage. A still pool warns of sickness. A sour smell marks bad water ahead. A root-scratch can guide a friend home or send a pursuer the wrong way.

Ordinary work fills the hours between dangers. Cooking smoke rises from sheltered fire-pits. Hide scrapers begin before the heat climbs. Skiff boards are sealed before raiding season. Children are taught early which clay pots near the elder’s post are left alone and why.

#### Morality and Social Bonds

Deyve morality begins with the household. A deed is judged by what it protects and what obligation it leaves behind. The same act can mean one thing for kin and another thing for a stranger.

The household comes first. A child belongs within the strength of the house. A mate is defended before outsiders are considered. An elder carries value because memory can save lives. A household that leaves its own exposed loses standing before the marsh and before every household watching.

Kindness in Dusk Reed carries weight. A favor can create claim. A rescue can create debt. Food given during hunger may return later as labor or silence. A gift can bind a person because everyone remembers who needed it and who gave it.

Tribal bonds are harder than household bonds. A Deyve may quarrel inside the tribe and scheme for rank. Ambition is expected. Betrayal before outsiders stains a name in a way ambition never does. Cruelty may be forgiven if it protected something. Exposure is harder to forgive.

Strangers stand outside most obligations until a household claims them. A merchant may be tolerated because trade is useful. A trespasser may be punished because punishment teaches others. Mercy can be shown, but in Dusk Reed it usually arrives with a purpose attached.

---

#### Speech and Dialect

Deyve speech keeps the old HobBog rhythm, but the rhythm has hardened. Words bend around rank and debt. A listener must hear what is being claimed as much as what is being said.

The running "s" appears often in ordinary speech. A Deyve may say, "Muds waitings for a foot." The phrase can warn a child or insult a stranger. When anger rises, the final sound may land harder. "Muds waitings for a foots" is closer to threat.

Deyve sayings are practical. A hidden rival may be called "roots with teeths." A patient enemy may be described as "still waters holdings hunger under." A fool who talks too much may be "a frog singings for the herons." A frightened liar has "fears showings through the reeds."

Conversation tests position. A simple question can ask who owns a crossing. It can ask who owes a debt. It can ask who has the right to speak first. Repetition gives the speaker time to measure the answer.

Affection is guarded but present. A mother may say, "You's stood good." She means pride and expectation at once. A mate may say, "I keeps your knifes dry." The words are plain, but the promise is intimate.

To speak like a Deyve is to speak with consequence. The words carry feeling, but they also carry claim.

---

#### Marriage and Family

Deyve courtship begins with proof. Beauty may draw attention, but pressure reveals value. A steady hand in bad water, a clean answer to insult, a dangerous crossing held without panic — these speak more clearly than open affection.

Love among the Deyves is tied to standing. A partner is judged by what they strengthen in the household and what danger they keep from the door. In Dusk Reed, desire alone fades before the first hard season.

Family bonds carry duty. A mate is defended and tested. A household may use a marriage to settle old debt. A strong joining can secure a passage. A weak joining can lower the standing of both houses.

Marriage gifts show control as much as generosity. A family may offer a weapon because it has strength to spare. A poison store shows access to guarded knowledge. A skiff share shows control over movement. A hidden landing may be the most valuable gift of all.

The joining is witnessed by elders and speakers whose debts are touched by the bond. The couple stands beside a marked channel, a root-post, or a low fire. Names are spoken so no witness can pretend confusion later.

A common marriage sign is the held passage. The pair crosses a narrow plank or root-bridge together. The crossing means they agree to hold one path through hunger and insult. If either stumbles, the witnesses remember. If both cross cleanly, the bond begins with approval.

Deyve marriages can hold affection, though it stays guarded in public. A good union gives the household more force without softening either partner. What begins in front of witnesses stays answered for by them.

---

#### Children and Games  

Older eyes measure how much danger a child can be trusted to carry. A young’un learns which pools sicken, which roots hold weight, and which voices to leave unanswered when they come from the wrong direction.

Play teaches position. A chase through the mangroves can become a test of hiding. A balance game on wet roots can become a test of nerve. A hiding game can become serious when one child claims a crossing.

One common game is called **Backward Walker**. Children mark false tracks in mud, turning the signs the wrong way to confuse the seeker. The seeker must read drag and weight rather than shape. A child who reads the mud correctly earns praise.

Other games teach claim. Young’uns build small crossings from sticks and argue over who owns the passage. They stain skiff scraps red and dare one another to touch sickly water with a pole-tip. They answer from behind roots so others learn to distrust an easy voice.

Deyve childhood is full of training disguised as play. A child who learns slowly may still be loved inside the household. The marsh makes its own accounting.

---

#### Death and Memory

Deyves believe the dead sink into the red mud beneath Dusk Reed.

At death, the final breath is taken by the marsh. The body returns through root and silt. The dead do not drift away from the household. They remain in the places their lives helped hold.

A strong death leaves weight behind. A feared mother may remain in the pool where she was returned. A raider may cling to the crossing named for him. A sick child who endured may be remembered at the door-post.

Deyves speak carefully near burial mud. They do not turn the dead into comfort. A good dead one should still have force. The dead may guard a claim or sour an enemy’s path. They may strengthen a household by reminding the living what must not be wasted.

After death, families may press red mud onto a skiff rail. They may mark a door-post with it. The mark does not ask the dead to rest. It asks them to remain useful.

To the Deyves, death is possession carried deeper. The body returns to the marsh. The name remains with the household. The strongest dead abide where their folk still dare to stand.

---

#### Songs

Dusk Reed songs make fear useful. A song can steady the hand before a raid. It can keep a child alert near bad water. It can carry warning without raising the voice.

Many Deyve songs are low because sound travels strangely through the roots. A raiding song may begin beneath the oars. It may tighten as the skiffs near a landing. A household song may praise someone by naming what they held when others failed.

Children’s songs carry the Backward Walker through the roots. The verses teach wrong tracks and false voices. A playful tune can turn cruel by the final line. The child learns that laughter ends quickly when the marsh answers back.

Work songs belong to poison-making and skiff repair. Their rhythms keep the hands steady. A root-fiber chant may teach a child when to tighten a knot. A poison song may count drops without saying too much aloud.

War songs rose after the Goblin Wars. Murkwillow singers praise hidden channels and decoy raids. Songs about the Crownless King claim that he broke the old order open. Mireborn songs are harsher because conquest is part of their inheritance.

Deyve songs rarely seek comfort. They honor the feared and mock the careless. A song can make a crossing into a warning. It can make a pool into a judge. It can make a voice in the mangroves into the last thing a fool follows.

### Cragwolds of the Weald

#### Overview

**Cragwolds**, known among themselves as the **Wyraks**, are a broad and enduring mountain folk shaped by the escarpments, forests, and stone ridges of western Taliria. They dwell throughout the Weald, Northern Forest Edge, and the high interior valleys where the mountain roads narrow into cliffs and the wyverns circle above the cloud line. To outsiders they are often grouped together beneath the broad term “Cragwold,” yet among themselves the old identities remain deeply important.

The mountain roads of the Wyraks climb through narrow ridges where a misplaced step can send loose stone into the valleys below. Fog settles heavily against the escarpments, hiding entire passes for days at a time, and many of the old ridge paths disappear beneath landslides or overgrowth before being cleared again by the mountain clans. Over generations the Cragwolds became a patient folk, known for patience, observation, and restraint. Their monasteries preserve engineering records beside astronomical charts and oral histories, treating each as part of the same long effort to understand the enduring patterns of the mountains above an ever-changing weald.

The Goblin Wars and the fall of Taliria deepened divisions that had long existed among the Wyraks of the western mountains. Generations earlier, during the Third Dynasty of the **Talirian Crown (TC)**, the **Weald Mark** (see below) was ceded from the province of Wyvern's Weald to the province of The Oasis Scourge so that the mountain valleys might serve as a stable refuge and source of cultivation, trade, and protection for the southern provinces during times of invasion or famine. Many Cragwolds embraced this duty and grew closely tied to the peoples of the Scourge, believing the mountains were meant to preserve not only their own folk, but all those who depended upon the Weald during times of collapse. Others viewed the ceding of the Mark as a dangerous bargain that bound the mountain clans too closely to the ambitions and failures of lowland kingdoms. To them, the mountains were no gift to be offered freely, but a harsh land that demanded patience, discipline, and understanding from those who sought refuge within them. Far to the west, more contemplative mountain clans withdrew from these disputes almost entirely, devoting themselves to astronomy, mathematics, engineering, and the preservation of ancient knowledge carried through the passes by trade and travelers. Though outsiders often speak of the Cragwolds as a singular people, the Wyraks themselves have never fully agreed upon what the mountains are meant to protect, nor whom they are meant to endure for.

Today the Cragwolds remain deeply rooted throughout the Southern Weald. Their roads still bind the mountain passes together. Their fortress archives preserve histories lost elsewhere in Taliria. Their stone halls still oversee the Wyvern Run. But beneath the outward calm of the mountains, old divisions remain unresolved.



#### The Land (of the Southern Weald)



<div class="map-card">
<img src="images/cragwold/wyverns-mark.webp" >
<p>The Southern Weald</p>
</div>

The Southern Weald is an enormous expanse including two mountain ranges on either side and a traversal range that separates it from its kin-northerners.  The traversal range is called Crown-Watch, made up of a vast network of mountain passes that stretch across the valley, separating the twin basins.  Crown-watch is home to an ancient Cragwold stronghold where many of the Cragwold nobility of The Weald make their home.   Here the political center of The Weald holds their courts.   While technically, the entirety of the Southern basin falls under Crown-Watch, it is mostly uninhabited as the Wyverns make life difficult in the Southern vale.  

The Western range is called the Ternwall Range; a large mountain range that runs northward from the Glentali region of Oasis Scourge all the way up to Northern Forest Edge.  Ternwall Range was home to the former Oasis Scourge's Ducal citadel, now in ruins atop the motte overlooking Glentali lake.   

The Eastern range drives south down near Dusk Reed all the north near the Embarkment of Northern Forest Edge and Middle Vale until veering Eastward; a strange directoral shift unexplained to this day.   Here you can find the Southern encarpments of WyrsSine and CragPine.

Lastly, a lone low , massive isolated massif, RidgeFount, standing isolated at the valley's mouth, acts as a natural bastion, forcing all northward travel into its shadow.

##### Ternwall Range and the Ternfolk

Along the western face of the Ternwall mountains dwell the Ternfolk, a contemplative and unusually isolated branch of the Cragwolds overlooking the harbor settlements of Ternhaven. Protected by the sea-facing cliffs and largely removed from the devastation of the Goblin Wars, the Ternfolk became known throughout the Weald for scholarship, astronomy, natural philosophy, and the preservation of ancient Talirian texts.

Unlike many other Wyraks, the Ternfolk maintain steady contact with traders and travelers arriving by sea. Foreign instruments, books, navigational devices, and fragments of distant philosophies often reach Ternhaven before they reach any other Cragwold settlement. Over generations this produced a culture less rigidly traditional than the eastern ridge clans, though no less disciplined.

The Ternfolk are often criticized by other Wyraks for detachment. Many Crown-Watch wardens view them as aloof observers who contemplate the world more than they defend it. The WyrSpine clans in particular distrust their fascination with outside ideas and foreign artifacts. Yet even their critics admit that much of the surviving mathematical and astronomical knowledge of southern Taliria endured only because the Ternfolk preserved it.

On the eastern side of the Ternwall range, only sparse pass settlements exist. These small mountain communities maintain hidden crossings between the western coast and the Weald interior, guiding travelers through narrow escarpments and ancient stone roads rarely used by outsiders.


##### RidgeFount and the Cultivator Clans


South of Crown-Watch basin, rises the broad central mountain known as RidgeFount, often called simply “The Fount” by the southern Weald folk. Wider and more fertile than the surrounding ridges, RidgeFount contains terraced valleys, glacial waterways, and cultivated highland settlements unlike any other Cragwold territory.

It was here that the Talirian crown forged its deepest alliance with the Wyraks.

When the Weald Mark were ceded to The Oasis Scourge, generations before the Goblin Wars, many RidgeFount clans embraced the decision as both strategic necessity and moral obligation. They believed civilization survives only when stronger regions accept responsibility for weaker ones. Over time, the RidgeFount Cragwolds became deeply tied to the peoples of Oasis Scourge, protecting trade routes, administering mountain crossings, and eventually overseeing the beginning of the Wyvern Run itself.

During the Goblin Wars, the RidgeFount clans opened their mountain settlements to refugees fleeing the fall of the south. Glenkin, humans, scattered HobBog survivors, and retreating Talirian forces were sheltered within the valleys of the Fount while armies marched through the Weald below. Vast quantities of food, iron, and mountain resources were poured into the southern war effort.

To many throughout Taliria, RidgeFount became the embodiment of sacrifice and civil duty.

To others, particularly the eastern WyrSpine clans, RidgeFount nearly doomed the mountains themselves.

Even today the Fount remains politically divided. Many RidgeFount folk still believe their ancestors chose correctly, preserving countless lives during the collapse of the south. Others quietly admit that the eastern warnings may have preserved the mountains had they been followed.


##### WyrSpine and the Old Wyraks

The eastern mountain chain known as WyrSpine rises beside the harshest wyvern territories in the southern Weald. Narrow ridges, broken escarpments, hidden caverns, and isolated stone holds dominate the range, producing a people hardened by generations of difficult survival.

Among outsiders, the WyrSpine clans are often called Spinefolk or simply eastern Cragwolds. Internally, many reject these names entirely and refer to themselves only as Wyraks — or more pointedly, as the Old Wyraks.

The WyrSpine folk believe kingdoms are temporary, but the mountains endure.

To them, the Goblin Wars proved the dangers of overextension and centralized ambition. Many eastern clans argued that the southern provinces were strategically indefensible once the Goblin King entered the Weald. Rather than pour resources southward, the WyrSpine elders believed the mountain passes should have been sealed and the Weald itself transformed into a defensive killing ground alongside the wyverns.

Had the Wyraks unified defensively, many eastern clans argue, the Goblin King may never have advanced north into the heart of Taliria.

This belief remains one of the deepest political wounds within Cragwold society.

The WyrSpine folk are deeply preservationist by nature. They distrust large roads, excessive mining, rapid expansion, and most attempts to reshape the mountains themselves. Isolation is not considered weakness among them, but wisdom. Many settlements remain hidden within ridge caverns and narrow cliff paths known only to local clans.

Yet despite their harsh reputation, the WyrSpine clans are not regarded as cruel among the Wyraks. Their severity is understood instead as discipline shaped by generations of survival in the eastern ridges. They simply believe survival requires restraint, discipline, and refusal to gamble the future of the mountains upon the ambitions of kingdoms.


#####  Crown-Watch and the Northern Wardens
At the northern threshold of the southern Weald stands Crown-Watch, the wealthiest and most politically powerful Cragwold stronghold of the region. Built among the northern mountains overlooking the upper passes, Crown-Watch guards the divide between the southern Weald and the northern territories beyond.

Unlike the contemplative Ternfolk or the isolated WyrSpine clans, the Crown-Watch Wyraks became builders, miners, engineers, and fortress lords. Their caverns descend deep into the mountains where iron, silver, and rare minerals are extracted through vast underground halls. Much of the surviving metallurgy of the southern Weald originated in Crown-Watch forges.

The Crown-Watch folk believe the mountains must serve civilization.

This philosophy places them in direct conflict with the preservationist instincts of the WyrSpine clans, who often accuse Crown-Watch of hollowing the mountains themselves. Crown-Watch responds that stone unused is stone wasted, and that strength, walls, discipline, and industry are the only things that preserved the Weald after the fall.

During the Goblin Wars, Crown-Watch coordinated northern defenses, maintained fortress roads, and organized retreat routes through the mountain passes. They also administered the conclusion of the Wyvern Run, receiving survivors at the great northern statue where contestants complete the crossing of the Weald.

Among many southern folk, Crown-Watch represents endurance itself.

Among others, it represents the slow transformation of the Wyraks into another ruling power little different from the kingdoms below. But in the end, they suffered as much as any of the other clans during the War, and it has hardened their nobility to side more often now with the clans of Wyrspine.


##### The CragPine Frontier
South of WyrSpine and east of RidgeFount stretches the vast woodland frontier known as the CragPine. Dense pine forests, lower mountain slopes, hidden grass valleys, and old migration trails dominate the region, creating one of the largest unsettled expanses remaining in southern Oasis Scourge.

Unlike the older mountain territories, the CragPine is still evolving.

Following the Goblin Wars, refugees, displaced Wyraks, scattered frontier clans, and wandering settlements slowly expanded into the forests where the wyverns rarely hunt. Over time the CragPine became a transitional society balancing mountain traditions against the practical realities of frontier survival.

Outsiders generally refer to the CragPine folk simply as Cragwolds. Internally, identity is less certain. Some still call themselves Wyraks. Others identify only as folk of the CragPine.

The frontier settlements maintain trade routes toward Middle Vale while carefully avoiding the deeper wyvern territories farther north. Many villages remain temporary by mountain standards, relocating after harsh winters, creature migrations, or forest fires.

Compared to the older mountain clans, the CragPine folk are unusually adaptive. They distrust rigid ideology, having survived the collapse of Taliria through flexibility rather than certainty. Some sympathize with RidgeFount. Others quietly agree with the WyrSpine traditionalists. Most simply seek stability in lands where stability rarely lasts for long.

Some among the southern bog folk now watch the expansion of the CragPine frontier with growing unease.

#### The Goblin Wars  

The Goblin Wars transformed the Cragwolds more deeply than many outsiders realize. Though the mountains themselves remained difficult for the goblin armies to fully conquer, the political consequences of the war fractured centuries of shared purpose among the Wyraks.

When Oasis Scourge came under threat, the cultivator clans of the central mountain descended from the heights to reinforce the south in accordance with their oaths to Taliria. Roads were opened, granaries emptied, and mountain retainers marched toward the frontiers of the kingdom.

The ridge clans believed this decision weakened the Weald itself. Many argued the goblin hordes could never have survived prolonged movement through wyvern territory and mountain passes had the Cragwolds withdrawn inward and allowed the mountains to become a death zone for invading armies.

Some among the ridge clans still claim the goblin king himself expected the Wyraks to retreat into the mountains rather than descend into the south. The decision of the cultivators to aid Oasis Scourge reportedly shocked even the enemy leadership.

That disagreement remains unresolved generations later.


#### Tradition and Lore

Among all Cragwold traditions, one belief remains nearly universal: the world decays when memory fails.

For this reason the Wyraks preserve mountain pathways long after kingdoms collapse. They maintain archives within mountain halls abandoned centuries earlier. They continue reciting star charts whose creators are long forgotten. Even ruined fortresses are rarely dismantled entirely, for many Cragwolds believe old stone remembers the intentions of those who shaped it.

This devotion to preservation shapes their relationship with nature itself.

Unlike the expansionist forestry traditions of the Alderdell, most Cragwolds reject large-scale transformation of the land. Forests are not resources to be consumed endlessly, but living balances that stabilize mountain life. Rivers are redirected carefully if at all. Entire roads are sometimes abandoned rather than widened through ancient pine valleys.

Among the Wyraks, permanence is not achieved through conquest. It is achieved through restraint. While the encroachment towards the bogs in the south, it remains a slow process as cragwolds honour and preserve the forest floor.

The mountains are believed to reveal truths that cannot be found in courts or cities. Rivers follow the shape of stone. Snow collapses where weakness already exists. Avalanches obey weight rather than desire. To the Wyraks, the world possesses an underlying structure independent of emotion or ambition.

This belief forms the basis of the One Way, a philosophical tradition practiced differently across the mountain ranges but centered upon alignment with the natural order of existence. Many Cragwolds believe civilization collapses when people attempt to impose themselves upon the world rather than understand it. Pride, conquest, greed, and excess are viewed not merely as moral failings but as forms of blindness.

The One Way values silence, endurance, restraint, and disciplined observation. Some Cragwolds spend decades preserving old mountain records, studying geometry, charting stars, or maintaining ancient roads not because such acts bring status, but because they preserve continuity against the chaos of passing generations.

The One Way does not teach that perfection exists beyond the world. To the Wyraks, truth is revealed within the world itself: in thaw and frost, flood and drought, birth and burial, the fall of stone and the return of green things after fire. Nothing living is meant to remain fixed. A thing becomes false when it refuses its season.

Among the older contemplative traditions, it is sometimes said:

> “The mountain does not bend to belief. Only to truth.”

The Cragwold sees the mountains vast repose as the embodiment of happiness. Their own fluid traversal life strives to replicate the mountain’s stillness.

This then all leads to their lore; as a typical recount of a lore-story:   


##### The Stone That Waited

<div class="lore-scroll" markdown="1">


When Hadrik was young, he wished to build a road.

Not a trail, nor a shepherd's path, but a true road carved through the High Pines of WyrSpine. He gathered companions, sharpened tools, and presented his plan before the elders.

"It will save weeks of travel," he said.

The elders listened.

"It will bring trade."

The elders nodded.

"It will bring prosperity."

The elders remained silent.

At last, an old woman named Sera placed a weathered map before him.

"Walk the valley first."

So Hadrik walked.

He crossed the High Pines and descended into the ancient valley where no road existed. The journey took many days. Fallen trees blocked the path. Moss covered forgotten stone markers. Snowmelt flooded sections each spring.

The road was needed.

When he returned, he told the elders what he had seen.

Again they listened.

Again Sera unfolded the old map.

This time she pointed to a faded line.

"There was once a road there."

Hadrik frowned.

"No road remains."

"Then find it."

The following spring he returned with workers and tools. They searched the valley and eventually discovered fragments of stone hidden beneath roots and moss. Entire sections had vanished beneath centuries of forest growth.

The road had existed.

For three summers they cleared it.

Not widened.

Not rebuilt.

Cleared.

Ancient retaining walls emerged from beneath the soil. Drainage channels still carried water after hundreds of years. Stone bridges stood where every young Cragwold believed there had only ever been wilderness.

When the work was complete, travelers crossed the valley once more.

The road served exactly as Hadrik had promised.

Trade increased.

Travel became easier.

The valley prospered.

Years later, after Sera had died, Hadrik finally asked why she had opposed his original plan.

An elder answered.

"She never opposed it."

"Then why send me away?"

The elder pointed toward the mountain above them.

"Because you wished to build."

He pointed toward the restored road.

"She wished you to remember."

The old Wyrak rested his hand upon the stone wall.

"A road can be built in three summers."

His fingers traced the weathered masonry.

"But this road carried the memory of those who came before us."

The mountain wind moved through the pines.

"If we forget them, we become the first generation."

He shook his head.

"And the first generation always believes it is the wisest."

For a long time neither spoke.

Far above, clouds drifted around the peaks.

The road remained.

The forest remained.

The mountain remained.

And Hadrik finally understood what the elders meant when they taught that permanence is achieved not through conquest, but through restraint.

The stone had waited.

It had lost neither its purpose nor its truth.

Only its witnesses.

</div>


##### The Wyvern

Another critical aspect of mountain tradtion and lore within the Weald is shaped by the apex predators, the Wyvern.  The mountains of the Weald are shaped as much by wyverns as by stone or weather. Wyverns are not worshipped among the Cragwolds, nor are they regarded as simple beasts to be exterminated. They are understood instead as ancient territorial forces bound to the high ridges and deep valleys of the mountain interior.

Entire settlements are planned around known wyvern flight paths. Children memorize the shadows and sounds of passing wings from an early age, and many mountain roads exist only because generations of Wyraks learned where wyverns refuse to nest or hunt.

Among the ridge clans especially, wyverns are often viewed as part of the mountain’s own balance. Some believe the mountains themselves reject those who attempt to dominate them, and that the wyverns merely enforce this older law.




#### Farming

Agriculture among the Cragwolds is centered upon **RidgeFount**, the great mountain basin whose streams, terraces, and fertile valleys sustain much of the Weald. While outsiders often imagine the Cragwolds as hunters, herders, and highland warriors, most know that the strength of the mountains rests first upon the harvest. RidgeFount's sheltered slopes produce grains, root vegetables, orchard fruits, and hardy mountain herbs in quantities unmatched elsewhere in the Weald. Its villages supply food not only to neighboring valleys but also to distant settlements scattered throughout the range.

Beyond RidgeFount, farming becomes more demanding. Along the escarpments and ridges, families carve terraces into steep hillsides where thin soils cling to the stone. Small communities tend hardy crops suited to the mountain climate while maintaining herds of goats that graze upon rocky slopes inaccessible to larger livestock. These villages rarely produce great surpluses, yet they are admired for their self-reliance and resilience.

To the Cragwolds, farming is not merely labor but stewardship. A field is expected to endure for generations, and a well-kept terrace reflects the discipline of those who built it. Elders teach that the mountains provide only what folk are willing to preserve, and thus farmers are held in high regard throughout Cragwold society. Though miners, craftsmen, hunters, and warriors all contribute to the Weald, many Cragwolds quietly acknowledge that RidgeFount's farmers form the foundation upon which the rest of mountain life depends.


#### Morality and Social Bonds

Cragwold morality is rooted in stewardship, restraint, and obligation. A Cragwold is judged less by what they claim than by what they preserve: a terrace kept fertile, a path held open, a promise remembered, a craft passed down, a mountain village protected through winter. Wealth and strength are respected, but only when they serve continuity. Recklessness, waste, vanity, and needless destruction are treated as signs of immaturity, because to damage the land or weaken a community is to burden future generations.

Social bonds among the Cragwolds are serious but not sentimental. Kinship matters, yet duty often extends beyond blood to village, craft-circle, ridge, herd, mine, song-line, or sworn companion. Elders, scholars, farmers, herders, and warriors each hold different forms of authority, but none are meant to stand apart from the whole. The highest praise for a Cragwold is not that they were bold, clever, or feared, but that others could depend on them when stone cracked, snow fell, or war came.


#### Speech and Dialect

Cragwold speech is deliberate and restrained compared to the faster and more emotional dialects of the lowlands. Many Wyraks consider excessive speech a sign of weak discipline, believing words should clarify meaning rather than conceal it. Long silences during conversation are common and rarely considered uncomfortable among mountain folk.

Their language contains unusually precise terminology for stone, weather, elevation, rope tension, snowfall, and mountain formations. Some dialects possess dozens of distinct words for cliffs, passes, ridges, and unstable terrain, reflecting the importance of geography within Cragwold life.

Among the contemplative traditions of Ternhaven, speech often becomes highly metaphorical, drawing comparisons between human behavior and natural forces.  

> “Snow reveals the branch already bent.”  

> “The cliff does not hate the falling man.”  

The ridge clans of the Weald speak more harshly and directly, favoring concise commands and practical observations shaped by generations of survival in wyvern territory.

#### Marriage and Family

Marriage among the Cragwolds is generally viewed less as romantic union and more as the joining of paths, households, and responsibilities. Many Wyraks believe affection grows through endurance rather than passion alone, and long partnerships built upon trust and labor are deeply respected throughout the mountain ranges.

Courtship traditions vary widely between the Cragwold traditions. Among the cultivator communities, marriages are often celebrated through large communal feasts tied to seasonal harvests and escarpment goat herd patterns. Among the contemplative mountain clans, unions are quieter affairs sometimes witnessed only by family elders or local wardens.

Children are typically raised withing the tyupical family unit and among a larger communal family, especially within the harsher ridge settlements where survival depends heavily upon shared labor and mutual protection.


#### Children and Games  

Cragwold children are raised with unusual independence from an early age. By the time most can walk safely along narrow cliff paths, they are expected to contribute to household labor, carry supplies, and memorize local mountain routes and wyvern warnings.

Children’s games among the Wyraks often reflect survival skills later required in adulthood. Stone-balancing contests, climbing races, memory games involving mountain paths, and silent tracking competitions are common throughout the Weald.

Among the ridge clans, children sometimes play games involving shadows cast by circling wyverns, testing how quickly players can disappear beneath cover when danger approaches overhead.

#### Death and Memory

Cragwolds rarely fear death in the dramatic manner of the lowlands, though they do fear waste, disorder, and the loss of memory. Many believe individual lives are temporary expressions of larger patterns enduring far beyond any single generation.

Burial practices differ across the mountain ranges. Some ridge clans leave their dead within high stone cairns overlooking ancient passes, allowing wind and weather to slowly reclaim the body. Others carve names and family histories directly into cliff walls or mountain halls preserved for centuries.

Among the contemplatives, it is considered dishonorable to exaggerate the deeds of the dead. To many Wyraks, truth is the highest form of remembrance.

#### Songs (of Stewardship and Cragwold Life)

Cragwolds are preservationists by nature, though they disagree deeply about what exactly must be preserved. Most believe the mountains, forests, and ancient passes possess an order that should not be carelessly altered. Mining, over-expansion, and the destruction of old forests are often viewed with suspicion among the Wyraks.

Some Cragwolds believe the forests strengthen and preserve the people who dwell among them. Others believe the Cragwolds themselves are guardians responsible for preserving the mountain wilderness against the excesses of the lowlands.

Both views continue to shape the songs one hears in the Weald.

### The Dellari Glenkin

#### Overview

Dellari history begins with an old westward migration, more than six centuries before the present, near the beginning of the First Talirian Dynasty. Glenkin families left the Verdant Steppes when Talirian expansion opened new lands across the realm, and some of the more adventurous families pushed all the way to Glentali Lake in Oasis Scourge. Their descendants did not remain eastern settlers. Over centuries, they became native to the basin. The lakeshore work shaped them — the ferry crossings, the canal maintenance, the fields that spread back from the water's edge and fed the whole province.

Glentali Lake became their homeland: a vast lake system fed by rivers flowing down from the southernmost reaches of the Ternwall Range. Their farms spread along the shore and the feeder waters. Ferries crossed between lake settlements. Life revolved around the water's rhythm — when to plant, when to repair the levees, how high the lake would rise and how long it would hold. They became the **Dellari — children of the basin** — a Glenkin culture that the lake had made its own over six hundred years.

Glentali's place in Oasis Scourge made the Dellari more than farmers. To the north stood the Ternwall Range, with the Cragwolds and human settlements of Ternhaven. To the south stretched the Shatterlands and its hard desert folk. To the east lay Greyharbor Downs and the bogs of eastern Oasis Scourge. The Dellari fed them all. Glentali's grain moved north. Its fish moved east. The ferrywork and canal labor tied every part of the province back to the basin. Over centuries, they also helped build and maintain the ducal citadel above the lake, the seat from which Talirian nobility governed Oasis Scourge.

When the Goblin Wars reached the Scourge, Glentali Lake became one of the first great prizes of the invasion. The basin was the system that made Oasis Scourge function — the water, the grain, the roads that moved both. When the goblin armies broke through, they did not merely burn farms. They shattered what held the province together. The ducal citadel was destroyed, the lake settlements were emptied, and the Dellari were massacred, enslaved, or driven north into RidgeFount and the mountain refuges below the Ternwall Range.

In the present day, the Dellari remain scattered between the homeland they lost and the mountain refuges that kept them alive. Some still look south toward Glentali Lake and the fields their families once tended. Others have grown into RidgeFount life, learning the mountain roads that survival demanded of them. They are the **Dellari — children of the basin** — driven from Glentali, hardened by exile, and still bound to the lake that gave them their name.

#### The Land

Dellari land is now divided between homeland, refuge, captivity, and dangerous return. Glentali Lake is their true homeland, but most of it is no longer under Dellari control. RidgeFount and the lower Ternwall slopes hold many of the free survivors. Salt-Willow shelters some through sympathy and shared hatred of Dusk Reed. Dusk Reed holds enslaved Dellari remnants taken during the war, born in captivity, or captured in failed return attempts.

Before the Goblin Wars, Glentali was a dense rural lake civilization. Its strength came from hommlets — not cities, but working clusters of kin-houses and ferry landings, fish racks and granaries, waterwork crews who knew which gate to open and when. Each hommlet was a piece of the basin. The basin only functioned because every piece held.

The fall of Glentali broke that system all at once. The citadel fell. The storehouses burned. The roads became unsafe the day the garrison scattered. Bridges went unrepaired. Levees failed without crews to watch them. Fields went untilled through seasons no one had the safety to plant. Lake piracy rose where ferries had run. Even after the main war passed, Glentali was not waiting to be reclaimed. It had become dangerous country.

Most free Dellari fled north and upward into RidgeFount, the lower Ternwall slopes, and the harsher mountain paths of the Weald. At first they fled the destruction of Glentali. Then the war followed them. As goblin forces pushed through Oasis Scourge toward Middle Vale, the Dellari were driven higher still, losing people to exposure and raid and the roads themselves.

Only the hardiest survived the diaspora. The weak hommlets failed. Dellari survival in the mountains demanded stubbornness from folk whose old world had been soft lowland soil and lake roads and the measured pace of canal work. Those who endured were not the whole of old Glentali. They were the fragments strong enough to remain.

The mountain diaspora is scattered hommlets rather than towns. Some sit near Cragwold settlements in RidgeFount. Some cling to Ternwall paths near Ternhaven. Some survive near Salt-Willow, protected by FogWisp sympathy and routes the Deyves have not yet found. A few hidden hommlets lie along the return roads toward Glentali, serving as way-stations for scouts and raider bands moving closer to the basin.

In Dusk Reed, another Dellari world remains trapped. Families taken during the fall of Glentali, their descendants, those captured in failed return attempts — all labor in the eastern marsh under Deyve authority. Their presence is a living wound among the free Dellari. The diaspora is also the knowledge that many of their own are still there.

The Dellari understand land as a chain of fragments. Glentali is homeland. RidgeFount is refuge. Salt-Willow is ally. Dusk Reed is captivity. The return roads are danger. What holds all of it together is the claim — still spoken, still named, still carried in household conversation while Ashverin militias hold the fields by force.

---

#### The Goblin Wars

The Goblin Wars destroyed the Dellari as a settled lake culture. Before the invasion, Glentali Lake was one of the most important regions of Oasis Scourge. Its farms fed the province. Its ferries tied shore to shore. Its canals and levees made cultivation possible. Its ducal citadel gave the basin protection and placed Glentali at the center of provincial rule.

When the goblin armies reached the Scourge, Glentali was too valuable to ignore. The invaders struck at the basin's food, movement, and authority. The citadel fell. Farms were burned. Lake settlements were emptied. Storehouses were seized or destroyed. Families were slaughtered, scattered, or bound for slavery. The Dellari were not merely defeated in battle. The structure that allowed them to live as Dellari was broken around them.

Many fled north toward RidgeFount and the lower slopes of the Ternwall Range. They did not flee into safety. They fled into a second hardship. The mountains were alien to lakefolk accustomed to fields, ferries, and basin seasons. Paths were narrow, winters harsher, soil thinner, and shelter uncertain. When goblin pressure continued through Oasis Scourge and toward the passes, the Dellari were forced higher still. Each rise saved some and killed others.

Those taken by Dusk Reed suffered a different fate. Many Dellari were enslaved in the eastern marsh, where Deyve power and the Crownless King's remnant order turned captives into labor, leverage, and inheritance. These enslaved Dellari remained distinct from the eastern Glenkin captives brought west from the old Verdant homeland. Both suffered under Dusk Reed, but they were not the same folk: one came from the fallen basin, the other from distant eastern lands.

After the war, the Dellari were too dispersed to reclaim Glentali quickly. The citadel was gone. The garrison was gone. The storehouses had burned or been seized. The waterworks sat without crews to maintain them. Families that tried to return in small groups found the roads dangerous. Others remained trapped in mountain hommlets, unable to move their people safely through ruined passes with the winter still holding.

A little more than a decade after the war ended, the Ashverin entered Glentali with organization, militia strength, Dusk Reed planning, and Pale Hymn support. They had been freed, tested, and remade through doctrine before being sent into the basin as a proxy folk. They arrived before the Dellari could gather strength. The Ashverin took farms, rebuilt settlements, raised militias, and claimed Glentali as their own.

From that moment, the Dellari struggle changed. They were trying to return to a homeland already occupied by another Glenkin culture — one with Dusk Reed backing, Pale Hymn networks, and the support of settlements from Greyharbor Downs to the eastern Shatterlands. The old basin had become a weapon against its own children.

#### Lore

Dellari tradition begins with hommlet life. A hommlet is more than a small settlement. It is every household that shares a water right, every obligation that runs between a family's boat and a neighbor's granary, every piece of the system that fails if someone stops holding it. Before the war, a Dellari child learned where the ferry ropes were kept, which levees belonged to which families, whose granary could be trusted in flood season, and which canal gates had to be watched when the lake rose.

Their oldest sayings come from work. A Dellari proverb is practical before it is beautiful. *A field remembers the lazy hand* means poor repair will punish the next season. *A ferry untied feeds no one* means good intentions without labor are useless. *Water finds the weak wall* means hidden faults reveal themselves under pressure. These sayings did not come from temples or courts. They came from the specific, daily consequences of a basin that only functioned when everyone held their section.

The oldest story told in Dellari hommlets is called the Levee Keeper's Bargain.

<div class="lore-scroll" markdown="1">

In the old telling, there was a levee keeper on the eastern arm of the basin who had maintained the same stretch of channel for forty years without the water ever breaking through. She knew every stone in the bank, every soft spot where silt moved at night, every joint that would hold and every joint that was lying to her.

One spring the water came higher than she had ever seen it. Not a flood — the lake does not flood out of spite. This was the patient kind of rising, the kind that tests what you built. For three days she walked the levee without sleeping, pressing mud, driving stakes, clearing debris from the outlet gates.

On the fourth night the water spoke to her. Not in words. In the way the current moved through a low joint she had shored up twice already.

She pressed her ear to the stone and listened for a long time.

Then she opened the gate herself and let the water through.

Not all of it. A measured cut. If she held the gate closed, the whole eastern channel would tear loose before dawn. Enough pressure to save the eastern channel and flood the low field instead. A family's winter grain went under that night. She went to them in the morning and said what had happened.

The family said nothing for a long time.

Then the elder said: "You chose us."

The levee keeper said: "I chose the lake."

The elder said: "They're the same thing."

She never argued that point. In the Dellari telling, she was right both times.

</div>

In exile, these traditions became portable. The Dellari could no longer maintain the great canals of Glentali, so they maintained what they could. A roof beam instead of a levee. A seed box instead of a granary. A footbridge over a mountain stream instead of a ferry crossing. The work grew smaller, but the ethic remained.

Their lore keeps the distinction between homeland and refuge. RidgeFount saved them, and many Dellari honor it. Yet RidgeFount is not Glentali. Dellari elders teach children the names of lake settlements they have never seen, ferry routes that no longer run, and fields now held by Ashverin militias. This is not nostalgia alone. It is a claim.

Among the mountain Dellari, new stories have appeared. Some tell of children who survived snow by sleeping beside Cragwold goats. Some tell of grandmothers who carried seed jars through three passes and never let them freeze. Some tell of hommlets that vanished after trying to return too soon. Trolls, crag-beasts, raiders, and Pale Hymn patrols appear in these stories less as monsters of legend and more as dangers that mark real roads.

The strongest Dellari tradition is endurance through usefulness. A Dellari earns standing by helping the hommlet last another season. Repair the bridge. Keep seed dry. Watch the pass. Hide the children when riders come. Teach the old lake names. Steal back a cousin from Dusk Reed. Carry news from Salt-Willow. A Dellari life is measured by what it preserves for the next hand.

---

#### Farming

Dellari farming began in Glentali's lake basin, where water made abundance possible and demanded constant management. A good Dellari farmer knew the soil, but farming was canal work as much as field work — reading the lake rise, timing the gates, keeping the shared waterworks repaired so that a failure on one family's stretch did not flood the next family's harvest. Farming and water management were the same practice.

The basin fed the province and beyond it. Glentali grain moved north to Ternhaven and through the mountain passes. Fish moved east toward Greyharbor Downs. The ducal citadel ate from Dellari stores. The road markets between the Shatterlands and the lake towns ran on Dellari seed stock. Their fields were dense family holdings — not grand estates, but working ground tied together by shared waterworks and the obligation to keep them running.

In the diaspora, farming became harder and more deliberate. RidgeFount soil is thin, broken, and steep. Mountain hommlets work terraces cut into slopes that were never meant for Dellari planting, coaxing harvests from ground that gives grudgingly. They learned from Cragwolds where they had to. A lake farmer forced into the mountains adapts or the hommlet fails.

Children learn early that mountain farming is survival by stubborn labor. Soil carried in baskets to fill a terrace. Seed protected through frost by whatever cover can be found. Stone walls repaired after every heavy rain before they wash the garden downhill. Harvests stretched through winter on less than the old basin gave in a single good flood season.

Some Dellari still keep old Glentali seed lines. These seeds are guarded with near-sacred care, not because the Dellari worship the seed, but because each jar represents continuity with the basin. A family that loses its seed has lost more than food. It has lost one more line back to the lake. In some hommlets, seed-keepers hold almost as much respect as elders, scouts, or healers.

Dellari raider bands and return groups often target Ashverin-held farms and storehouses. Some steal grain because the hommlet needs it. Others go specifically for what cannot be replaced — a canal map, a water ledger, a seed jar that still carries an old Dellari mark. A stolen sack of grain feeds a hommlet for weeks. A recovered canal map can guide a future return. A seed jar bearing an old Dellari mark can bring an elder to tears.

#### Morality and Social Bonds

Dellari morality is built around obligation, repair, and rightful belonging. A good Dellari shows loyalty through labor. Mending a neighbor's roof after rain. Sharing seed when a family's jar runs low. Standing a watch so someone else can sleep. Empty pride is disliked. Boasting without usefulness is what children do before they learn better.

The hommlet is the center of social life. A family line matters, but no line survives alone — exile made that truth impossible to ignore. Dellari leadership tends toward the practical: an elder who knows which families are still kin, a scout who knows which pass is safe after frost, a seed-keeper who can feed the hommlet through a failed harvest. The folk who hold standing are the folk the hommlet depends on to still be alive next season.

Their deepest anger is reserved for betrayal of kin and place. Dusk Reed is hated for enslavement, massacre, and manipulation. The Ashverin are hated in a more complicated way. They are Glenkin, but they hold Dellari farms, claim Dellari lands, and serve a doctrine that rewrites rightful belonging. To the Dellari, the Ashverin are not strangers who conquered them. They are cousins remade into occupiers.

This does not make all Dellari cruel. Many pity the Ashverin's origins as captives. Some elders still say the eastern captives were broken before they were sent into Glentali. Yet pity rarely survives an Ashverin patrol, a seized field, a burned hommlet, or a cousin taken back to Dusk Reed. The feud is intimate because it is Glenkin against Glenkin.

Cragwold relations are grounded in respect earned through endurance. The Cragwolds did not become Dellari saviors in any soft sense. They offered hard refuge, hard lessons, and sometimes hard judgment. Dellari who survived the higher paths gained respect because they had no natural right to survive there and did anyway.

Salt-Willow relations are warmer. The FogWisps understand being surrounded by enemies and threatened by Dusk Reed power. Salt-Willow routes shelter some Dellari, carry messages, and help keep the truth of Glentali alive. The Dellari know the bogs are not their homeland, but many owe FogWisp families their lives.

#### Speech and Dialect

Dellari speech is softer than Cragwold speech and less slippery than HobBog speech. It carries the rhythms of farming, ferrywork, and hommlet life. Old Dellari phrases root themselves in basin life. Even mountain-born Dellari may speak as if the lake is still just beyond the next road — referencing canal weather, seed condition, the feel of a bank that wants to give way.

They use practical comparisons. A difficult person may be *a gate swollen shut*. A secret that will not stay hidden is *water under a weak wall*. A reliable friend is *a rope that held*. A useless promise is *a ferry with no oars*. Dellari insults tend to cut through usefulness. To call someone *unmended* means they cause trouble others must repair. To call a family *dry-handed* means they take from shared work and return nothing.

Exile has altered the sound of Dellari speech by region. RidgeFount-born children speak with Cragwold terms woven in — stone and pass and goat-path words their grandparents never needed. Those who grew up near Salt-Willow carry wetland phrasing: bog routes, skiff calls, the shorthand of folk who move through hidden channels. Those who came out of Dusk Reed may refuse certain marsh words entirely, or use them with a flatness that signals where they learned them.

Dellari do not speak of Glentali as a vanished place. They speak of it as occupied, broken, or held. An elder might say *our south fields are under Ashverin watch*, though no one in the hommlet has walked those fields in twenty years. This habit is deliberate. To speak of Glentali only as lost is to surrender the claim.

The phrases that move through a Dellari hommlet are short and carry weight. *Keep the gate* is said to anyone who looks ready to walk away from a responsibility that still needs holding. *Water finds the weak wall* is the reminder that what was patched badly will fail in front of everyone eventually. A Dellari who makes a promise with nothing behind it gets told *a ferry untied feeds no one*, and that tends to end the conversation. *Carry seed, not sorrow* is what an elder says after a death when there is still planting to do. The most common phrase, the one said so often in some hommlets that children pick it up before they understand it: *the basin is held* — Glentali is occupied ground, and the Dellari are still the ones it belongs to.

---

#### Marriage and Family

Dellari marriage is built around household joining, shared work, and survival. Before the war, a marriage joined more than two families. It might tie two farms that shared a canal boundary, or a ferry family to the family that held the landing rights, or two seed lines that had worked neighboring ground for generations. Love mattered, but so did competence and the ability to strengthen the hommlet.

In exile, marriage became more urgent and more careful. The Dellari population is scattered, and many family lines were cut apart by massacre or slavery. Marriages often involve long travel between mountain hommlets and Salt-Willow refuge pockets. A wedding may also serve as a reunion of cousins who rarely see one another, a chance to confirm which families still live.

A Dellari spouse is expected to work. What counts as work covers the full range of what keeps a hommlet running — scouting a pass, watching a goat pen, hauling water up from a mountain stream, teaching children the old lake names so the knowledge passes to the next hand. A household that cannot contribute becomes a burden in hard country, and the Dellari are honest about burden.

The joining itself is marked by the Crossing. The couple steps together across a levee before their families and the hommlet's elders — a real levee, either maintained by one of the families or built for the occasion. The act means they will share the weight of the bank between them. What breaks on one side is both their problem. After the Crossing, families feast and the elders speak the names of both households going back as far as memory holds.

Family names often preserve Glentali origins. A name may carry the ghost of a farm, a ferry landing, an island, a gate, or a section of shoreline now held by Ashverin militias. These names are claims carried in the body. A Dellari child may bear the name of a place they have never seen, and be expected to know why it matters.

---

#### Children and Games

Dellari children grow up quickly. In safe times, Glentali children once learned through chores near water. A child old enough to carry a message was already useful. A child who knew which bank was dangerous after rain was already trusted. Games imitated work because work was everywhere and early mistakes near water cost someone something real.

In the diaspora, children's games are sharper. They play gate-mender, building small walls from stones and testing them with poured water. They play ferry-run, racing between marked points while carrying sticks as cargo. They play Ashverin watch, where one child patrols and others try to move seed bundles unseen. Near Salt-Willow, children learn hiding games from FogWisp children. Near RidgeFount, they learn climbing games from Cragwold neighbors, though Dellari elders often watch these with alarm.

Many games teach survival without announcing it. A child who can move quietly, count the rations, and carry a message through three checkpoints without repeating it aloud has learned something that will matter later. Play becomes training because the Dellari cannot afford helplessness.

Stories for children often begin with Glentali, but not always as grief. Elders tell of ferry races, foolish goats on levees, fish so large they broke baskets, children who fell asleep in grain carts, and grandmothers who scared off tax men with spoons. The point is to teach that Glentali was a living place, not only a wound.

Children born in RidgeFount sometimes know the mountains better than their elders do. A grandfather may know every canal of a lost lake, while his granddaughter knows which ridge path is safe after frost. Dellari culture survives by letting both kinds of knowledge matter.

The hardest children are those rescued from Dusk Reed. Some remember captivity. Some remember nothing before it. Some speak with Deyve turns of phrase or flinch at songs other Dellari consider harmless. Hommlets that take them in must decide how much to ask, how much to teach, and how quickly to bind them back into Dellari life.

---

#### Death and Memory

Dellari death customs once belonged to the lake. The dead were washed, named, and buried on family ground — a rise above flood reach, the edge of an orchard, a slope above the shore where the water would never reach. Families visited graves during planting and harvest, bringing a meal, repairing the marker, speaking aloud what had happened since the last visit. A grave was part of the hommlet's continuing life.

The war shattered these customs. Many Dellari died without burial. Many were taken east and vanished in Dusk Reed. Many died in mountain flight, on roads, in passes, or in hidden camps where stopping long enough to bury them properly would have doomed the living. This has left a deep wound in Dellari practice. They are a folk with too many unfound dead.

In exile, Dellari use name-boards, seed jars, and stone markers when bodies cannot be recovered. A name-board may hang in a hommlet hall or under a roof beam, listing those lost at Glentali, those taken by Dusk Reed, and those who vanished on return roads. Seed jars sometimes bear the names of dead family members, especially if that person carried seed from the basin or saved it during flight.

Mountain graves are difficult for older Dellari. Some accept them. Others resent them. To bury a Dellari in RidgeFount is to admit that exile has become real enough to hold the dead. Yet refusing burial helps no one. Over time, many hommlets have created small Dellari grave terraces, often facing south toward Glentali when the land allows it.

The enslaved dead in Dusk Reed remain the hardest grief. Free Dellari often do not know who has died there, who still lives, or whose children were born in captivity. This uncertainty shapes their hatred of Dusk Reed. A dead relative can be mourned. A vanished relative remains a command.

Dellari remembrance is not passive. To remember a person is to keep a duty. If a grandfather died repairing a bridge, the bridge must be named. If a mother vanished on a return road, someone must still watch that road. If a child was taken east, the family must leave a place at the table when news comes. The dead do not ask for monuments. They ask that the living continue the work.

---

#### Songs

Dellari songs began as work songs. A planting chant kept the row straight. A ferry call kept the oars pulling together. A levee rhythm steadied a crew through mud and dark. Most were short and repetitive, designed to keep bodies moving in the same direction at the same pace. A good Dellari song was one that got the work done.

After the fall, the songs changed. Some became quieter because noise could attract danger. Some became walking songs for mountain paths. Some became naming songs, moving through old lake settlements so children would learn them before they were old enough to wonder why. Some became rescue songs, sung only in trusted company, carrying references to Dusk Reed captives and roads that move south.

Dellari songs rarely praise kings. They praise the neighbor who shared seed when the harvest came short, the rope that held when it should have snapped, the rain that arrived on the right day, and the fool who forgot to tie the ferry and let everyone know it. Even grief songs return to work. A song may begin with the fall of Glentali and end with someone mending a roof. That is Dellari temperament: sorrow is real, but the roof still leaks.

A gate song teaches a child the canal duties before the child is old enough to work the gate alone. In the mountains, walking songs took over where ferry songs left off, carrying groups through passes at a pace that keeps courage up and complaints down. Name songs move through the hommlet at night, reciting old lake settlements and family lines that would otherwise be lost to a generation that never saw them. And then there are the songs that travel in quieter company — the captive songs carried by those who still have kin in Dusk Reed, and the return songs that scout bands sing on the roads moving south, short enough to stop mid-verse and resume without losing the thread.

A well-known Dellari refrain:

> Hold the rope and mend the wall,
> Carry seed though kingdoms fall,
> Lake below and stone above,
> Bring them home by work and love.

### The Ashverin Glenkin

#### Overview

The Ashverin came to Glentali as a wounded folk remade for settlement, labor, and obedience. They began as Glenkin of the Verdant Steppes, taken during the war years when villages fell and the old roads filled with prisoners. Their first shared memory is household fracture: parents counted away from children, siblings divided by age and strength, kin scattered into labor columns and sale wagons.

Dusk Reed hardened them before the Pale Hymn shaped them. Captivity taught silence, caution, and the terrible discipline of staying useful. The ones who bent and endured drew attention. The bent stalk endured became their bitter wisdom, then their doctrine, then their pride.

Near the end of the Goblin Wars, Dusk Reed opened the gates for a chosen portion of these captives. Pale Hymn attendants stood beside the release columns with white cloth, grain bowls, water basins, and ledgers of new names. The Ashverin remember this as the Unbinding. Dawn light, pale bands on trembling wrists, seed pressed into the hand — these are the images their ceremonies return to again and again.

The mercy carried a design. Dusk Reed gained loyal settlers. The Pale Hymn gained a wounded folk ready to be renamed. Glentali gained hands for fields, canals, storehouses, and ferry routes. The Ashverin were sent into the basin with seed, tools, animals, clerks, and ritual overseers. They were told the land had been waiting for a remade folk.

They worked well. They repaired channels, reopened ferries, raised levees, planted abandoned fields, and filled the granaries. Glentali rewarded their labor, and the reward became proof. The Ashverin looked at the grain, the water, the straightened roads, the ducal citadel bright with pale banners, and saw confirmation.

Beneath that confidence sits fear. The Dellari remember the basin before Ashverin hands were planted there. The hill and mountain Glenkin remember older lines of kin and slope. Every older memory threatens the Ashverin story. Grief in them has been trained into severity, and severity has been trained into rule.

#### The Land

Glentali Lake is the only homeland most Ashverin have touched with their own hands. The Verdant Steppes live in elder fragments, old weather words, and lullabies that slip out at dusk. Glentali is the place of work, marriage, burial, tax, and song. The Ashverin hold it with the intensity of folk who received land after humiliation and fear losing the meaning of their survival.

The basin under Ashverin management is orderly and productive. The old Dellari canal system runs again through repaired gates and cleared channels. Ferry crossings move on schedule. Levee crews answer seasonal calls from the meeting halls. Fields that grew wild after the Goblin Wars have been replanted, terraced, and tied back into the lake.

Their settlements have a deliberate shape. Ashverin hommlets gather around meeting halls, granary yards, band houses, and work ledgers. The streets serve labor before beauty. The ducal citadel rises as the ceremonial heart of the basin, a place where palace rites make local rule feel blessed by distant authority.

The eastern roads bind them to Dusk Reed. The southern routes connect them to Pale Hymn trade through the mudflats and Shatterlands. The mountain approaches trouble them most. Those roads lead toward Dellari hommlets, Cragwold sympathy, and the kinds of memory the Ashverin prefer to keep beyond the basin rim.

They speak of the Dellari as fringe folk and of the Cragwolds as hill folk with loud stone-pride. This confidence is genuine and rehearsed. Ashverin children learn the basin by gate, field, and ferry before they learn the old claims against it. By the time they hear those claims, the land already feels settled inside them.

---

#### The Taking

The Verdant Steppes Glenkin were taken during the war years, when raids and military collapse made whole villages into movable property. Captured households were sorted after the raids in counting yards and guarded camps. Names were copied badly, then replaced by marks, tallies, and assigned uses. A family could vanish in one afternoon through the simple act of being counted into different columns.

Those who reached Dusk Reed entered a system built around use. The eastern marsh understood captive labor, food control, and the slow breaking of local memory. Work crews were shifted between reed plots, fish channels, mud roads, and holding yards. Children learned new commands before they understood why their old ones brought punishment.

The proud drew harm quickly. The furious were used as warnings. The ones who bent survived the longest. Some received better sleeping places, more reliable rations, and responsibility over other captives. Those rewards cut deep because they were real. Survival began to feel like a moral lesson.

The Pale Hymn moved through that pain with terrifying patience. It gave words to endurance and shape to shame. It told the captives that the old household had failed, that the Many could hold what blood had lost, that a new name could end the terror of being only what had been taken. Those who accepted the Hymn earliest rose first.

By the time the first Ashverin cohorts were gathered for Glentali, they were already selected. They were capable, organized, and grateful in the manner power had taught them to be grateful. Their obedience had fed them. Their discipline had preserved their children. Their belief had become the room where grief could stand upright.

They received a name, a basin, and a purpose. Hold Glentali for the Many. Feed the order that freed them. Prove through labor that the Unbinding had meaning. They arrived in the lake country with that purpose burning brighter than any memory older than pain.

---

#### The Pale Hymn

The Pale Hymn is the architecture of Ashverin society. It shapes marriage, burial, work crews, food distribution, speech, discipline, and rank. Every hommlet has a meeting hall. Every meeting hall has name ledgers. Every name ledger carries the quiet threat of being seen clearly by the Many.

In Glentali, doctrine takes the form of water and grain. The canal keeper serves the Hymn. The ferry master serves the Hymn. The seed keeper, levee crew, granary clerk, and field band all serve the same sacred labor. The many sow. The many reap. The many eat. The many remember.

The palace ceremonies give the Ashverin their highest form of recognition. Grand Cantors arrive from far provinces beneath pale banners, escorted by clerks, singers, ash-bearers, and guards. The ducal citadel fills with grain-gold lamps. Ashverin bands crowd the courts while the Unbinding Rite, the Pale Naming, and the Rite of the Clean Furrow are performed before the settlement.

These rites tell the Ashverin that suffering had purpose. They tell children that obedience made their grandparents strong. They tell farmers that the basin accepted their hands because their hands were cleansed for use. Each ceremony turns private hurt into public certainty.

The bands carry that certainty into daily life. They begin as labor crews and become guardians of standing. A band repairs a gate, escorts a tax cart, watches a road, questions a stranger, and corrects a neighbor with the same calm conviction. Band leaders gain influence before age grants it. Young Ashverin seek them because bands turn belief into rank.

Pale Hymn in Ashverin hands is agricultural, severe, and hungry for confirmation. It praises the shared table while watching who eats. It blesses the common field while counting whose hands work slowly. It sings of freedom while tightening every custom around the folk it claims to have freed.

---

#### Lore

Ashverin lore begins in Glentali because Glentali is the part of the story their society can bear to repeat. The first repaired ferry. The first harvest after the eastern levee held. The grandmother who kept the seed dry through the flood season. The band leader who guarded the pass until reinforcements came. These are the stories told openly.

The older past sits deeper. Elders carry it in habits more than speech. A pause before answering. A glance toward exits. A hand that gathers bread quickly before thought catches up. Younger Ashverin see these things and call them age, temperament, or old Dusk Reed manners. The elders let those answers stand.

<div class="lore-scroll" markdown="1">

The story is told of an old man whose first name was left outside the telling.

He had worked the eastern marsh for so many years that his grandchildren thought the reed plots were the shape of the world. His back bent toward the water. His hands carried old scars from rope, gate, tool, and winter hunger. He had learned the phrases required of him. He had learned the songs. He had learned the face that made overseers pass him by.

One autumn, a Pale Hymn clerk came with a Dusk Reed captain and said there was a place in the west that needed steady hands.

The old man asked what kind of place.

The clerk said: a basin.

The old man asked what kind of hands.

The clerk said: hands ready for the Many.

The old man looked at his grandchildren, then at the reed water, then at the strip of pale cloth folded in the clerk's palm.

He asked what would be left behind.

The clerk answered: everything that kept you bent.

The old man laughed once. It sounded like a cough.

He crossed into Glentali with the first cohort. He farmed the eastern parcel for twenty-two years. He kept the southern levee alive through seventeen flood seasons. He died in his bed with three generations around him, which was more mercy than his youth had taught him to expect.

Years later, a traveler asked his youngest grandson where the Ashverin came from.

The boy pointed to the lake and said: from here. From the basin. From the place the Many gave us.

The old women nearby kept singing and lowered their eyes.

</div>

Their proverbs carry the same wound dressed as wisdom. *The bent stalk endured. A crooked root must be lifted early. Grain remembers the hand that works it. A name held too tightly becomes a wall.* These sayings sound practical, and they are. They also teach obedience before the listener feels the lesson enter.

Below the proverbs survive fragments from the Verdant Steppes. A counting rhythm from dry fields. A word for rain that makes little sense beside Glentali Lake. A lullaby shared by women from different hommlets who swear they learned it from different grandmothers. These fragments are small enough to pass beneath doctrine, which is how they live.

---

#### Farming

The Ashverin farm Glentali with skill. They inherited a damaged Dellari water system and made it run. Gates were repaired, channels cleared, banks raised, ferries timed, fields divided, and storehouses restored. Their competence gives their claim its strongest voice.

Farming is also ritual. Seed is issued through meeting halls. Harvest crews move by communal order. A household may tend a plot, but the plot belongs first to the basin and the basin belongs first to the Many. The granary teaches doctrine more persuasively than any sermon because it feeds children.

The Ashverin respect yield above memory. Old Dellari seed lines sometimes appear in abandoned margins, and Ashverin farmers plant them for what they produce. They see Dellari seed jars as grief made into an object. To an Ashverin farmer, a seed proves itself by feeding mouths.

Their hidden debt runs through the canals. Much of what they understand about flood timing, gate rhythm, and feeder behavior came through captive Dellari knowledge in Dusk Reed years. A Dellari hand taught an Ashverin hand when to open a gate and when to wait. Official history thins that exchange into Ashverin diligence. The water remembers more than the ledgers record.

This makes Ashverin farming both admirable and ugly. They work hard. They feed the basin. They also turn the fruit of inherited knowledge into evidence against the folk who carried that knowledge first.

---

#### Morality and Social Bonds

Ashverin morality begins with usefulness to the Many. A good person strengthens the field, the hall, the band, the household, and the ledger. Private loyalty has value when it serves public order. Private grief becomes acceptable when shaped into communal memory.

The bands enforce this morality before magistrates need to speak. A band sees who lingers near strangers, who questions a levy, who eats alone too often, who repeats an elder phrase with too much feeling. Correction may arrive as advice, work assignment, public song, or sudden attention. Most Ashverin understand the warning before harsher measures appear.

Their contempt for the Dellari has a cold quality. Heat would dignify the claim. The Ashverin call Dellari kin-searchers thieves, raiders, hill claimants, and holdout folk. Criminal language makes the conflict feel like maintenance. Maintenance can be handled by patrols, ledgers, and rope.

Dusk Reed occupies a harder place in Ashverin moral life. The official story calls Dusk Reed the hard passage before deliverance. Many Ashverin honor it as a stern teacher. Older Ashverin carry a different truth in their bodies. They have learned which truths earn silence and which truths earn safety.

The tragedy is plainest here. Ashverin society rewards the exact shape captivity carved into them. Caution becomes wisdom. Obedience becomes purity. Gratitude becomes rank. They were wounded, then rewarded for the shape of the wound.

---

#### Speech and Dialect

Ashverin speech carries Pale Hymn grammar deep into ordinary talk. Collective terms feel natural to them. They say the field, the harvest, the crossing, the basin. A child learns these forms before understanding doctrine, so doctrine feels like proper speech.

Possessive language still exists, but it narrows under pressure. My house becomes the household. Our grain becomes the hall share. A personal complaint becomes a matter for correction, because speech has already moved the self into public hands.

Elder speech carries older textures. Some words come from the Verdant Steppes, especially for dry wind, thin rain, dust, hunger, and distance. Younger Ashverin hear these as quaint old phrases. A few hear them as a door.

Band speech is sharper. It uses Hymn terms as markers of rank and belonging. A band member can say clean furrow and mean moral purity, field order, social obedience, or a warning to someone standing too close to Dellari sympathy. The words carry several knives.

Their Dellari vocabulary is deliberately small. Dellari claims are old claims. Dellari elders are hill elders. Dellari hommlets are holdouts. Dellari roads are fringe roads. The language moves the Dellari backward in time before any argument begins.

---

#### Marriage and Family

Ashverin marriage is confirmed in the meeting hall before witnesses and ledgers. The ceremony joins two households as a new contribution to the Many. The couple receives blessing through grain, water, and ash. Their hands are pressed together over a shallow bowl of basin soil while the officiant names the duties that will bind them.

Family affection can be real and tender, but the household serves a larger order. Marriage through family preference alone carries suspicion. A good match strengthens labor, improves standing, joins useful skills, and places children inside reliable networks. Love may grow there. Duty arrives first.

The Pale Naming enters family life early. Children receive names before the hall and learn the names of released ancestors as sacred inheritance. The old Verdant naming customs survive mostly as whispers, pet names, and elder slips during illness or grief. When such a name surfaces, the room often grows careful.

Large households carry honor because they offer more hands to field, hall, and band. A child is cherished as a child and counted as future service. This double vision gives Ashverin family life its uneasy warmth: love under ledger, tenderness under doctrine, lullabies under the sound of communal song.

---

#### Children and Games

Ashverin children grow inside the Hymn from the first remembered song. The meeting hall is nursery, school, court, and festival ground. Children carry messages, sort seed, learn gate names, fetch water for work crews, and practice the earth-pressing gesture before they understand why adults watch the form of their hands.

Their games train order. One game sends children running as canal crews, opening and closing imaginary gates while others pretend to be floodwater. Another has them carry grain tokens from household to hall while band youths judge fairness and speed. The games feel lively, but they teach coordination, obedience, and the habit of being measured.

Dellari children appear in lessons as figures from a sad wrong road. Teachers say the Dellari lost the basin, fled to hills, and returned as claimants after other hands restored the work. This story gives Ashverin children pity before curiosity. Pity is safer than curiosity.

Elders sometimes break the pattern. A grandmother hums a tune from a dry country. A grandfather calls a child by a name that belongs to a vanished line. The child may remember the sound years later, after the hall has taught many cleaner answers. That is how doubt begins among the few who develop it: as music, as tenderness, as a word spoken once and then swallowed.

---

#### Death and Memory

Ashverin death returns the body to communal ground. The graves are aligned, even, and well tended. Each stone gives the name recognized by the hall. Each name-board carries the dead into harvest rites, name-givings, and Unbinding ceremonies. The dead remain useful through example, memory, and warning.

The burial ground has little family distinction. That is the point. The dead belong to the Many, and the living are taught to meet grief as a shared duty. A mourner who kneels too long at one stone may receive company. Company is comfort and correction at the same time.

Older customs survive in gestures too small for doctrine to catch. Some elders press one palm to the earth at canal junctions, field borders, and ferry landings. It resembles the formal Hymn gesture, but the rhythm is different. The hand touches, waits, and withdraws as though greeting someone under the soil.

Ashverin death is orderly, dignified, and lonely. It gives every life a place in the community and takes away the wild privacy of grief. The same wound returns at the grave: what was personal becomes useful, what was loved becomes public, and the Many sings over the place where a family might have wept alone.

---

#### Songs

Ashverin songs have three layers. The highest layer is formal Pale Hymn: unison voices, steady rhythm, communal meals, name-givings, harvest nights, and palace rites. When a large settlement sings across the lake, it sounds like certainty made audible.

The second layer belongs to Glentali itself. Canal crews sing gate songs. Ferry hands sing timing chants. Children sing of floodwater, ducks, levee mud, and the smell of spring rain moving over the basin. These songs are local enough to feel innocent, and that innocence makes them powerful. They teach the Ashverin that Glentali has always been the shape of their life.

The third layer lives in old throats. Women hum dry-country lullabies while mending. Men use counting rhythms from the Steppes while stacking reed bundles. A tune crosses three hommlets with different words and the same sorrow beneath them. These songs survived because they were too small for officials to fear.

Band songs have the hardest edge. They are sung on patrol roads, near Dellari approaches, and in palace yards after rites. They praise clean furrows, steady hands, held ground, and the strength of those who bend together. The young sing them with pride. The old hear the iron under the melody.

The Ashverin sing beautifully. That beauty is part of the horror. The voice that mourns has been trained to march. The lullaby has been taught to serve. The Hymn takes pain and gives it harmony, and the harmony makes the pain easier to obey.

### The Pale Hymn

#### Overview

Pale Hymn began after the Goblin Wars, when the Talirian Kingdom was broken and the old provinces had no common throne left to answer to. Its first teachers came south from Ashen Pale, carrying a doctrine of brotherhood into lands that had lost families, farms, roads, rulers, and names. They did not arrive as conquerors at first. They arrived as listeners, singers, healers, record-keepers, and organizers of hungry towns.

The message was simple enough for the wounded to understand. No folk should stand alone. No field should feed one house while another starved. No child should inherit only the quarrels of their elders. No village should cling to a dead past when the many could build a common future.

That is how Pale Hymn speaks when it first enters a place.

What lies beneath the name is the collective. What lies beneath the collective is the earth. What lies in the earth, the Pale Hymn does not name directly.

---

#### Origins

After the Goblin Wars, the Ashen Pale did not empty.

The battles that ended the invasion left behind a silence that visitors describe differently depending on when they entered and what they were carrying when they did. Some speak of an unsettled weight in the ground. Others of voices that arrive as feeling rather than sound. What most agree on is that the Pale after the war felt less emptied than waiting.

From that silence, the first teachers emerged — folk who had survived the war in the Ashen Pale and found, when the armies withdrew, that they could not fully leave what the war had left behind. They built a doctrine around what they found there: the dead do not depart. They return to the earth and continue to hold claim on the living. The individual who dies becomes the collective that endures. To serve the Many is to honor the dead who built it. To hoard name, land, or labor for oneself is to steal from those already beneath the ground.

From the Ashen Pale, the doctrine moved southward into devastated Talirian lands where the Goblin Wars had shattered the old order and left populations without households, without elders, and without the structures that had once organized their lives. It did not need to break anything. The war had already done that.

---

#### The Doctrine

Underneath the promise is a harder order. Pale Hymn does not merely ask folk to share labor. It teaches that the individual is incomplete until joined to the many. A household that remembers itself apart from the whole is called selfish. A name that resists correction is called a wound. A custom that keeps old loyalties alive is treated as a seed of future division.

This is why the doctrine stands opposite the Cragwold way. The Wyraks remain upon their mountains and test strength against stone, weather, memory, and restraint. They believe truth is found by enduring the world as it is. Pale Hymn does not wait on the mountain. It creeps into every crevice below it. It enters ports, farms, refugee camps, taverns, shrines, granaries, schoolrooms, and marriages. Where the Cragwolds preserve, Pale Hymn revises. Where the Cragwolds endure, Pale Hymn recruits.

---

#### How the Hymn Moves

Pale Hymn changes its language wherever it settles. In the **Shatterlands**, it speaks of scorching. The old plains must be burned clean so the saved may cross without carrying the dust of former claims. Among desert converts, fire becomes proof. A person is not purified by memory, but by surrendering what memory kept alive.

In **Glentali**, Pale Hymn speaks of harvest. The flow of water, labor, grain, and ferrywork becomes the image of salvation. The many sow. The many reap. The many eat. The many remember. The Ashverin were taken from the Verdant Steppes as slaves during the Goblin Wars, sold through Dusk Reed, and reshaped by the doctrine into a people with a new name and a new purpose. That purpose was Glentali. The doctrine tells them they have always belonged there. Their hands work the fields, their grain fills the storehouses, and their allegiance proves the truth of their claim. The Dellari memory is not denied outright. It is thinned, corrected, and renamed until it becomes a refugee story instead of a homeland claim — until the people who actually built Glentali become a footnote in the history of those who replaced them.

In **the bogs**, Pale Hymn speaks more softly. It does not begin by insulting the boglet. It tells young HobBogs that kinship can be wider than root, skiff, hut, and family story. It teaches them new greetings over food and water. It asks them to mark themselves by shared allegiance rather than household memory. A young FogWisp may still speak the old dialect, but the obligation underneath the words has changed.

Pale Hymn therefore does not look the same in every land. In the Shatterlands, it burns. In Glentali, it harvests. In the bogs, it widens kinship until the boglet loosens around the young. Its promise is brotherhood. Its method is replacement. Its final demand is that no one belong to themselves before they belong to the many.

#### Beneath the Hymn

The dead hold a quiet place in Pale Hymn, though its teachers rarely explain this to new converts. Officially, the dead are witnesses to the common future. Their names are sung so no sacrifice is wasted. Their ashes may be mixed into boundary clay, granary seals, or memorial ink. Their bones may be kept beneath meeting floors or within pale shrines where the faithful gather to sing.

The doctrine says this preserves the fallen within the many.

Its older Ashen Pale teachers say less. They know remembrance can become use. They know a body returned to the common order has not fully left it. In most towns this remains hidden behind song, ash, and ritual language. No convert is asked to name what it is. They are only taught that the dead still serve.

---

#### Signs in the World

A traveler can recognize Pale Hymn presence by several markers before any doctrine is spoken.

Converts address each other by given names, not birth names, family terms, or titles tied to household or boglet. The old name is not renounced in public. It simply stops being used until it is forgotten by everyone who once knew it.

Food is distributed communally before any household takes its share. The ritual marks who belongs to the Many and who does not. A guest who eats at a Pale Hymn table without understanding the gesture has already participated in it.

When crossing a threshold into shared space, adherents press both palms briefly to the earth — honoring the collective dead below. The gesture is quiet. Outsiders rarely notice it the first time.

Speech avoids possessive forms when addressing collective property. *The field.* *The harvest.* *The crossing.* Not *my share* or *our water.* Among converts this is so natural it no longer sounds deliberate.

Songs are sung in unison, without individual lead voice or call-and-response. The Hymn does not allow one voice to carry what belongs to all.

The new name is announced at a communal meal, not in private. The old name is not spoken again by those who witnessed the meal.

---

#### The Appeal and the Cost

This is what makes Pale Hymn intoxicating to the young. It gives them importance before they have earned standing at home. It tells them their doubts are not laziness, rebellion, or shame, but proof that they were born for a wider order. It gives them phrases that make them sound older than their parents and cleaner than their ancestors.

It can also move the old. Parents who survived war, hunger, exile, and local feud may hear in Pale Hymn the promise of rest. The doctrine offers common stores, common defense, common teaching, and an end to the small cruelties of household rivalry. To a tired mother, a shared granary may sound like mercy. To a father with no sons left, the many may sound safer than blood.

The cost comes later.

Pale Hymn does not destroy the self in a single demand. It pares it down through usefulness. First the household mark becomes less important than the common sign. Then private grief is corrected into public memory. Then old names become provincial errors. Then refusal becomes sabotage. By the time a person understands what has been taken, the many are already speaking through every neighbor.

---

#### Reach

The Pale Hymn has moved steadily southward from its origin in the Ashen Pale, following war damage, displacement, and populations with no household left to return to.

- **Ashen Pale** — origin and deepest stronghold; the doctrine holds uncontested
- **Glentali** — most complete expression of Pale Hymn material power; Ashverin have reshaped the entire lake basin
- **The Shatterlands** — one established city; traditional desert folk resist and can articulate why
- **The Mudflats** — transit corridor between Dusk Reed's eastern channels and the western bogs; doctrine moves through it in both directions
- **Reedveil** — seeping in from the mudflats; young FogWisps are the primary convert population; elders are worried and divided
- **Weald edges and Free Cities** — travelers and rumors only; no organized presence yet

---

#### Opposition

The **Dellari** resist because the doctrine erased their kin, renamed their homeland, and calls that erasure the completion of what they began. There is no Pale Hymn version of this story in which the Dellari are not a prelude.

The **traditional Shatterland desert folk** resist on philosophical and historical grounds. They have their own account of the dead, the earth, and what the collective means — and it does not require surrendering the name. Their resistance is the oldest and most articulate in the south.

**Salt-Willow** resists because boglet obligation is the foundation of FogWisp life. The Pale Hymn dissolves the boglet into the Many. There is no FogWisp culture without the household, the debt, and the particular mud one belongs to. A FogWisp who gives their obligation to the Many has, in Salt-Willow's understanding, stopped being a FogWisp.

FogWisp community also depends on the individual coming into their own within the boglet. A strange habit, a crooked song, a private craft, a new telling, an odd joke, a painted skiff, even a foolish scheme may become useful because it came from someone in particular. The community grows by making room for expression, then testing what that expression gives back.

Pale Hymn cannot tolerate that for long. It may praise local color at first, and it may borrow the words of the place it enters, but its deeper movement is always toward sameness. The oddity must become a sign. The private song must become a common hymn. The self must be made useful to the many before it is allowed to exist at all.

The **Cragwolds** are the ideological counterpoint. The mountain philosophy holds that strength is built through individual and clan endurance against the land's hardness. Pale Hymn teaches that the mountain is a wall built to keep the Many from reaching each other. These two doctrines do not negotiate.

### Songs of Salt-Willow

#### Introduction

Music occupies a central place in the cultural life of Salt-Willow. It preserves stories, carries memory between generations, strengthens communal bonds, and gives voice to emotions that are often difficult to express directly. A FogWisp may learn as much about their people through song as through formal teaching, for the songs of the marsh carry lessons about home, belonging, loss, resilience, and the living world that surrounds them.

Across the settlements of Salt-Willow, music accompanies nearly every aspect of life. It can be heard drifting from taverns at dusk, sung quietly aboard fishing barges moving through the reeds, shared among families gathered around lantern light, or carried across the water during communal celebrations and seasonal rituals. Songs are remembered, adapted, and passed onward, allowing each generation to remain connected to those who came before.

The themes that emerge throughout FogWisp music reveal a people whose identity is inseparable from the marsh itself. The waters, trees, reeds, creatures, and fog are not merely scenery but active participants in the emotional landscape of the songs. Home is understood not as a collection of buildings or boundaries but as a living relationship between people, place, and memory. When a FogWisp sings of the swamp, they are often singing of family, ancestry, community, and self all at once.

Many songs also reveal a remarkable balance between hardship and warmth. Even when confronting grief, displacement, danger, or uncertainty, FogWisp music rarely descends into despair. Humor, stubborn optimism, and quiet resilience continually surface, reflecting a culture that endures difficulty through community rather than conquest. The songs celebrate ordinary lives, small victories, familiar creatures, and local traditions with the same reverence that other civilizations might reserve for kings, wars, or great monuments.

Taken together, the music of Salt-Willow offers a unique window into the FogWisp worldview. These songs do more than entertain. They preserve cultural memory, reinforce shared values, strengthen communal identity, and reveal how the people of the marsh understand their place within the wider world. Through them, Salt-Willow emerges not simply as a setting, but as a living civilization with its own emotional voice.

<div class="song-box">




*Cattails* is one of the foundational songs of Salt-Willow. Nearly every FogWisp would know its refrain, often learning it long before understanding why it carries such emotional weight.

> “You can take me anywhere
> But I’ll find my way back home.”

At first hearing, the refrain sounds simple. Its repetition feels comforting, almost stubborn in its certainty. Yet beneath that certainty lies a deeper tension. The promise of return carries meaning only because separation is possible. The singer insists they will find their way home because there is always the fear that distance, time, war, or change might one day place that home beyond reach.

For the FogWisps, home is not merely a location. It is the marsh itself, the people who inhabit it, the rhythms of daily life, and the generations who came before. To return home is to return to oneself. The repeated lyric therefore becomes more than a declaration of direction. It becomes an act of faith.

The song also reflects a recurring theme throughout Salt-Willow culture: persistence. The cattails continue to grow after storms. The waters continue to flow. The people continue to endure. No matter where life carries them, the connection between the FogWisps and their homeland survives. Even when the future feels uncertain, the song insists that some bonds remain unbroken.

What gives *Cattails* its emotional power is the coexistence of comfort and sadness. The listener feels the warmth of belonging, but also the quiet awareness that belonging can be threatened. The song does not ignore loss. Instead, it answers loss with determination, offering reassurance that memory, identity, and attachment can survive even when the world changes around them.

#### The Frog Who Lost His Swamp

<div class="song-box">


*The Frog Who Lost His Swamp* pairs an intricate musical arrangement with a remarkably intimate perspective. Through a soaring operatic performance, the listener experiences the marsh through the eyes of a frog who slowly begins to notice that something around her no longer feels the same. The changes are subtle. The water no longer hums. The cattails no longer whisper. Familiar sights and sounds seem increasingly distant.

The arrangement gives extraordinary weight to these observations. As the frog moves through memories of the marsh he once knew, a flute weaves throughout the song, remaining close to the vocal line and carrying many of the emotions left unspoken. Together, the voice and flute create a piece that feels both beautiful and melancholy, transforming a simple story into something far more affecting.

What makes the song particularly powerful is that it never explains exactly what has happened. The listener is given only the frog's perspective and the small changes he notices around him. The loss could of been his log was moved by a current or one of those big HobBogs. What matters is that the swamp no longer feels as it once did.

The emotional climax arrives in the final chorus:

> “Oh my swamp,
>
> My swamp,
>
> What have they done to my home”

The prolonged crescendo on *to my home* lifts the final phrase above everything that came before it. By the song's conclusion, the listener understands that the frog is not mourning an abstract place, but something deeply personal. The song leaves open the possibility that the loss itself may be small, yet to the frog it carries enormous weight. In doing so, it captures how even the slightest change to something cherished can feel profound to those who belong there.

#### Cypress Queen

<div class="song-box">


*Cypress Queen* tells the story of a mysterious pixie of the swamp whose cypress grove comes under threat from loggers seeking timber and profit. Confident in their plans and armed with axes, they enter the wetlands believing the marsh can be measured, marked, and claimed. The Cypress Queen has other ideas.

The arrangement mirrors this playful struggle. The music twists and wanders like a hidden path through the wetlands, moving with a sense of mischief and motion. Even as the danger facing the cypress trees remains real, the song maintains an almost playful confidence, as though the outcome has already been decided long before the first tree is marked.

The heart of the song lies in the Queen herself. She is clever, patient, and deeply connected to the land she protects. Her goal is not destruction but correction. The loggers leave soaked, exhausted, and thoroughly humbled, carrying with them a lesson they failed to learn when they first entered the grove.

By the final verses, the cypress trees remain standing while the intruders retreat through the mud. The Queen's laughter mingles with the creaking of ancient branches overhead, turning the grove itself into a participant in the story. What begins as a tale of threatened trees ends as a celebration of a swamp that knows how to defend itself.

</div>







---

#### Root and River

```{r, results='asis'}
cat('<audio controls>
  <source src="songs/hobbog-saltwillow/Root and River.mp3" type="audio/mp3">
</audio>')
```

<div class="song-box">



*Root and River* stands in contrast to *Cattails*. Where one song speaks of return, this one confronts the possibility that return is no longer possible. The singer is not merely separated from home. The fen itself has been taken away, leaving behind a sense of loss that cannot be repaired through distance, time, or distraction.

The song's brevity contributes to its impact. There is no long story, no explanation, and no resolution. Instead, the listener is given a handful of powerful lines that linger long after the music ends. The swamp remains present in memory even as it slips further from reach.

> “Ale can’t drown the swamp’s dark song.”

The lyric captures the persistence of belonging. The singer may try to escape the pain, but the marsh remains. Its sounds, memories, and rhythms continue to echo beneath everything else.

The emotional climax arrives with the song's final word:

> “Nowhere.”

By this point, the loss extends beyond the fen itself. Home is gone, and with it the certainty of where one belongs. The song leaves the listener suspended between memory and absence, creating one of the most melancholy moments found within the music of Salt-Willow.

</div>



---


#### Cypress Bones

```{r, results='asis'}
cat('<audio controls>
  <source src="songs/hobbog-saltwillow/Cypress Bones.mp3" type="audio/mp3">
</audio>')
```


<div class="song-box">


*Cypress Bones* occupies a different place within FogWisp storytelling than songs of community, celebration, or longing. It is a warning carried through song, telling of a dryad bound to an ancient stand of cypress trees and the outsiders who continue to press deeper into the swamp in search of timber. The song begins with restraint, yet beneath that restraint lies an unmistakable threat. The dryad is not asking for mercy. She is offering a final chance to leave.

The atmosphere feels ancient and watchful. The swamp is presented as a living domain whose memory stretches far beyond the lives of those who enter it. The dryad stands as both guardian and voice of that memory, watching as greed and carelessness push ever closer to the heart of her grove.

Rather than focusing on conflict itself, the song lingers in the moments before it. Warnings are given. Boundaries are crossed. The listener is placed alongside those who continue forward despite every sign that they should turn back. This growing sense of inevitability gives the song much of its tension.

A recurring idea runs throughout the piece: the swamp remembers who harms it. The dryad's patience feels immense, shaped by seasons and centuries rather than days. Yet the song makes clear that patience is not endless. Every cut into the grove carries a cost, and every warning ignored brings consequences closer.

> “You’ll feed the cypress bones”

Indeed.  The raspy warning whispers that trees need nutrition.

Among FogWisps, *Cypress Bones* serves as a reminder that the generosity of the swamp should never be mistaken for weakness. Beneath the still waters and quiet cypress stands rests an ancient resolve that protects what belongs there.

</div>



---

#### Conclusion

Taken together, the songs of Salt-Willow reveal a bog-folk who find meaning in things others might overlook.

A frog grieving a familiar stretch of marsh. Toads elevated into legendary guardians. A dryad defending ancient cypress trees. A traveler who can no longer find a way home. None of these stories concern kings, empires, or great wars. Yet each is elevated to a grander hierarchy.

Again and again, the songs return to the same idea: the places they love become part of who they are. A pond, a cypress grove, a stretch of a shifting islet, or a patch of reeds may seem small to outsiders, but within the songs they carry the weight of entire worlds.

The result is a musical tradition that feels deeply rooted in Salt-Willow itself. The marsh is not merely the setting of these stories. It is their foundation. Every song emerges from it, returns to it, and draws meaning from it.

In listening to these songs, one begins to understand the FogWisps themselves. Their humor, grief, loyalty, stubbornness, and affection for home all flow from the same source.

The swamp endures even as it shifts.

And so do the stories.
